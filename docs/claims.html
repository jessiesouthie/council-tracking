Eagle Mountain · Fact checks

What's going around, and what the record says

Things residents are telling each other about the city: the tax increase, the staffing, the petition, all set against the meetings, the notices and the audited figures they can be checked against. Some are true, some are out of date, and the ones the city itself put into circulation get the same treatment as the ones aimed at it.

29 claims checkedLast reviewed Oct 23, 2026

Each check, with its sources

Overtaken by eventsChecked Aug 19, 2026 · updated Oct 2, 2026

A 220 percent property tax increase

“The council raised your property taxes 220 percent.”

220.88% was noticed in June and never adopted. The rate that survives is 0.000900: 69.81% over the certified rate, on the city's own line, which is about a tenth of a property tax bill.

Accurate, but incompleteChecked Aug 19, 2026

Every dollar of the increase goes to public safety

“Every dollar of the increase goes straight to public safety.”

True in the schedule's own terms: the levy is smaller than the Sheriff's contract, so there is no second dollar to allocate. What it left out is that the money largely replaces sales tax already paying that contract, and frees the sales tax for other things.

Unsupported by the recordChecked Aug 19, 2026

The tax was rushed through in secret

“They did this in secret and rushed it through.”

Two different charges are tangled together. On whether it was done in the open the record is unusually complete: eight open meetings from April 14, both August hearings set three months ahead, the schedule read into the record five times. On whether residents found out, the council agrees with the criticism: three of the five said so by name from the dais before voting, and a fourth voice said it in the cross-talk that followed.

Not accurateChecked Aug 19, 2026

The money pays for a city police department

“The increase pays for the city's own police department.”

Eagle Mountain employs no police officers and no firefighters. Patrol is bought from the Utah County Sheriff's Office; fire and ambulance come from Unified Fire Authority, which bills residents on a separate line of the same tax notice.

Accurate, but incompleteChecked Aug 19, 2026 · updated Oct 7, 2026

Signing the petition repeals the tax

“Sign the petition and the tax goes away.”

Signing doesn't repeal anything. It's the first of three steps: enough valid signatures put the increase to a public vote, and only a majority voting no repeals it. The petition going round now is the second one; the first was aimed at a rate the council has since repealed.

Another data center is being built

“They're building another data center in Eagle Mountain.”

Yes, and the votes are on file. What is being built is more of what is already here: every data center approval in the record since 2024 is an expansion by Meta or QTS, not a new company arriving.

It will be the largest data center in the country

“It's going to be the largest data center in the country, maybe the world.”

No. A single project already approved in Box Elder County covers 40,000 acres: more ground than the whole of Eagle Mountain, which is 32,256 acres of land. Whatever gets built here, the largest in the country is not going in this city.

Accurate, but incompleteChecked Aug 21, 2026 · updated Oct 3, 2026

A $150 million tax break for the data centers

“You gave the data centers a $150 million tax break and now you are asking residents to pay.”

The break is real and the council was a party to it, but the $150 million was granted by five taxing entities together and Eagle Mountain City's own line is about six cents of every dollar, roughly $9 million of it. What the claim leaves out cuts both ways: the deals struck since 2018 are worth several times $150 million, and the city's share of the newest one alone is larger than twenty years of the tax increase.

Accurate, but incompleteChecked Oct 6, 2026

Data centers will heat up the land and water around them

“According to that professor, I think it's Utah State, it's going to rise the heat of the land around it and the water, and that's going to affect wildlife, the environment, and people.”

Data centers do warm the air next to them: the one direct measurement found about 1.5°F on average, up to 4°F, reaching about a third of a mile. The Utah State professor's much larger figures were for a 9-gigawatt project in Box Elder County, not Eagle Mountain, and no one has measured anything here.

Accurate, but incompleteChecked Aug 21, 2026

A developer was let out of building the rodeo grounds

“The developer was supposed to build the rodeo grounds out in Pole Canyon, and the city let them out of it.”

The promise was real, it was in a signed contract, and it had a date on it: about twenty acres of public rodeo grounds, built and dedicated by June 1, 2015. The city did release it: nine weeks after residents stood at the podium and asked the council to enforce it. What the claim leaves out is that the release was a noticed agenda item carried 3–2 with two members voting no, and that the city took something in exchange each time it gave ground.

Not accurateChecked Oct 7, 2026

The mayor's pamphlet argument was filed late

“Your argument should have been excluded from the packet as it was a day late.”

The mayor's argument is stamped received on September 8, the fifteenth day after the referendum application was filed. State law gives the sponsors and the city fifteen days each, so it arrived on the last day allowed, not after it. The argument the city's pamphlet marks late is the sponsors' own.

Without the increase, police and fire lose funding

“The lie that without the tax increases the firemen and police will suddenly lose their budgets they already have in place.”

What it leaves out is where the money would come from instead, and that fire is due to move onto the city's books in 2028.

Accurate, but incompleteChecked Oct 7, 2026

The city published sponsors' home addresses

“You have printed out and mailed extremely expensive card stock covered packets to every city resident with our signatures and addresses that were not redacted as other cities have done.”

The city's pamphlet does carry the sponsors' home addresses and signatures, and the liaison's phone number and email, unredacted. State law requires the pamphlet to include the application, and the application to contain those details, but Salt Lake County blacked them out of its own pamphlet in January. This page found no record that Eagle Mountain mailed printed copies to every household.

Not settledChecked Oct 7, 2026

The vote was pushed to August to keep turnout low

“These delay tactics to push us into an August election that very few voters will turn out for shows your true colors.”

The August date is real: the petition the city issued names a special election on August 10, 2027. Whether the law allows it is open, and one statute reads against it: a city may call a special election on a tax question only in November. Why the city chose August is not something the record shows.

MisleadingChecked Oct 8, 2026

The lower rate was negotiated in private

“When a mayor individually approaches citizens challenging a tax increase through a referendum, and a state senator helps negotiate a compromise with that group, which then withdraws its referendum, the public deserves to know how those negotiations occurred and whose interests were represented.”

The mayor and some councilmembers did meet privately with sponsors of the first referendum before August 18, and one of those sponsors said so at the meeting. But the record shows no rate settled there: it was argued out and voted on in the open meeting. And the first petition wasn't withdrawn as part of a deal. State law voided it the moment the rate it targeted was repealed.

Accurate, but incompleteChecked Oct 23, 2026

Sponsors can't circulate until they sign a city agreement

“We still cannot legally circulate this petition. The city is holding us hostage over a new statutory requirement that must be put into writing with the city.”

The requirement is real: state law bars the sponsors from gathering signatures until they sign an agreement with the city recorder. The city’s draft, now public, covers the two things the law asks for and adds several terms the law doesn’t mention. Whether the city is refusing to drop them, and so holding up the petition, is still only the sponsors’ account.

MisleadingChecked Oct 23, 2026

The city’s petition agreement strips sponsors’ rights

“To print our packets we have to sign a contract that waives our constitutional right to a jury trial, grants the city total governmental immunity, and severely limits our legal remedies while shifting liability onto citizens.”

The draft does have a jury-trial waiver and a cap on what the sponsor can recover, but the post describes them as more than they are. The jury waiver binds the city too, the immunity clause only says the city keeps the immunity state law already gives it, and nothing in the draft makes a sponsor liable for anything. Adding terms beyond the two the law lists isn’t barred by the statute.
