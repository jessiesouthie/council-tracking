Eagle Mountain City Council, 29 October 2024

What happened

Written from the meeting recording, not from the approved minutes.

In short: Eagle Mountain's council and staff sat down with regional planners for a briefing on the Transit Fresh Look study, a joint effort by eight cities and several transportation agencies to plan new transit for northern Utah County and southwest Salt Lake County. No decisions were made. The city's representatives pushed back hard on the draft route maps, arguing the plan bypasses the Ranches and ignores Pioneer Crossing, and the consultants agreed to revisit those lines.

Present: The transcript does not record a roll call, so individual attendance cannot be confirmed. Elected officials and city staff spoke throughout, but the recording does not identify them by name.

Staff: Lauren Victor (long-range planner, Wasatch Front Regional Council — attending remotely because of illness); Liz Scanlon (Kimley-Horn, the consultant firm facilitating the study, presenting the slides).

Decisions

No motions were voted on.

Settled without a vote:

  • The consultants agreed to take Eagle Mountain's objection back to the project team and re-examine whether the main east-west corridor should follow the lakefront alignment or Pioneer Crossing, after city representatives said the current draft skips the Ranches entirely.
  • The consultants acknowledged the concern that a route along 2100 North functions as a freeway and would not attract riders, and said they would "go back and talk that out" alongside suggestions for Pony Express and Porter Rockwell.
  • The consultants accepted that a proposed yellow line should be extended to reach the American Fork FrontRunner station, which the current map does not show.
  • No direction was given on corridor width preservation beyond the consultants' commitment to work through right-of-way constraints with city staff over the following weeks.

What was discussed

Transit Fresh Look — study purpose and partnership

The meeting was a briefing on the Transit Fresh Look study, a regional planning effort to work out what it would take to bring more transit into southwest Salt Lake County and northern Utah County. Liz Scanlon of Kimley-Horn, the consulting firm facilitating the work, explained that the study deliberately steps back from past efforts. "We know that transit has been studied and talked about a lot, and there might be a little bit of fatigue about that," she said, but the team is "endeavoring to kind of step back and bring new thoughts to the table."

The study is run as a partnership rather than a top-down plan. The contract sits with the Wasatch Front Regional Council, but the partners include the Mountainland Association of Governments, the Utah Department of Transportation, the Utah Transit Authority, and eight cities: Lehi, Saratoga Springs, Bluffdale, South Jordan, Herriman, Riverton, Eagle Mountain, and West Jordan. Scanlon stressed repeatedly that the consultants would not push a conclusion the cities did not share: "if there's not feeling good or there's not good vibes or we're just a little bit nervous, we really want to understand that."

The work runs in four stages — diagnostic, discovery, refining, and blueprint. The team started under contract in April with data collection on existing conditions and city land use plans, held a brainstorming session in July where participants drew lines on maps, and met again in September to narrow those ideas into corridors. The next partners meeting was set for November 18, with the blueprint stage — a roadmap for how to actually build any of this — coming in the new year.

Goals and transit-oriented development

Scanlon walked through the goals the partners set at the outset: better connections within the valley and to the wider region, improved access for people who struggle to get around, economic development that fits each city's own general plan, and above all a plan that gets used. There was, she said, "a really, really important emphasis on an implementable plan. Like not a planning study we throw on a shelf."

She then explained the ingredients of transit-oriented development, which she grouped as density, distance, design, diversity of land uses, and destinations. She was careful to defuse the word density: "we're not talking about high rises and anything inappropriate," she said, but rather modest development — ground-floor shops with a couple of storeys of housing above — clustered near stations. She also flagged uses that work against transit, naming car washes and gas stations as poor neighbours for a transit line, and suggested cities think about what happens as those properties turn over.

A recurring theme was the split in control. Cities control zoning, where development goes, and station access — sidewalks and bike connections. Regional agencies control service types and frequency. The goal, she said, is transit that beats driving: "I can ride the transit vehicle and get on Wi-Fi or get on my phone and work or read a book or sleep versus sitting on I-15 in traffic."

Corridor maps and mode choice

The presentation included a draft Utah County corridor map that Scanlon described as "hot off the press" and headed to the November partners meeting. The lines were scored against performance measures drawn partly from federal funding criteria, since new transit in the region has historically depended on federal money, and partly on access to FrontRunner, future land use, and rough cost and feasibility. She was blunt about the limits of the exercise at this stage: "we don't want to set you up for failure to the degree that, gosh, we drew a line and it has to go across Utah Lake."

The next phase moves from where the lines go to what runs on them. The study will weigh local bus, bus rapid transit, and light rail. Scanlon described bus rapid transit as "light rail light" — a bus with dedicated stations, sometimes its own guideway, branded vehicles and off-board fare collection, similar to the UVX service in Orem and Provo. Light rail is far more expensive; a fixed-route bus is the cheapest starting point.

Questions from the body

The first question asked whether the study looked at corridor widths that need to be preserved not just for bus rapid transit but for future light rail. Scanlon answered "yes and no" — the team has collected what cities have already preserved, and is only now starting detailed right-of-way conversations with city staff. A speaker in the room pressed the point sharply: "The most expensive part of transit is the land," and buying corridors in the wrong place wastes taxpayer money, while waiting means tearing down homes and businesses later. "So the sooner we can get that, the better."

A second question asked whether the study is looking at what has to be built first — whether the routes have a sequence, or would be built piecemeal wherever pressure appears. Scanlon said phasing discussion started in September and is common practice: build a fast bus, watch ridership grow, upgrade to bus rapid transit, and eventually light rail, while preserving the corridor from the start. She singled out Eagle Mountain as offering "a really amazing opportunity here to align general plan, zoning and land use in these transit corridors together." She also corrected an assumption from the floor that a central corridor project is under construction — the grey line on the map covers corridors under construction or with a locally preferred alternative selected, which is a stage of study, not a shovel in the ground.

The Pioneer Crossing and Ranches objection

The sharpest exchange came over what the draft map leaves out. A speaker noted that at a staff meeting the previous month, Pioneer Crossing had shown as the highest-ranking corridor, and asked why it no longer appears. Others in the room piled on: Pony Express has a gap through the Ranches, and the high-capacity route as drawn bypasses the Ranches completely, running down Ranches Parkway instead. "There's thousands of cars coming through Saratoga Springs on Pony Express," one speaker said. "I would love to see some of those people on buses, but this is showing the high capacity route, bypassing the ranches completely."

The frustration was partly about money already spent. "We got $1.3 billion to fix Pioneer Crossing and there's not even no kind of plan for bus rapid transit," a speaker said, calling the map "so confusing" given that Pioneer Crossing is the busiest road. Another objected to routing transit on 2100 North on the grounds that it is effectively a freeway, and freeway buses lose: "People don't want to take the bus when they're sitting on the bus on a freeway and people are going past them 80 miles an hour in a car." The alternative offered was a route that beats congestion rather than joins it — "if you're on Pioneer Crossing and you're going on a bus and you're passing everyone who's in gridlock traffic, you're like, oh yeah, I'm doing right."

Scanlon explained how the map got that way. After July's brainstorm, the team ran performance measures on the ideas, then re-workshopped the lines at the September partners meeting, and this map came out of that discussion. She acknowledged the lakefront-versus-Pioneer-Crossing question "was a whole lot of debate in that meeting" with Utah County city representatives and remains unsettled. She also explained the Mountain View Corridor line: Salt Lake County partners see a large gap in north-south transit between I-15 and the west side, and want to know where such a line could connect in Utah County — potentially at an intermodal hub in Saratoga Springs feeding the Eagle Mountain lines. On Pioneer Crossing, she conceded the point: "I appreciate the comment that maybe we don't have this line quite right... We'll go back and talk that out."

Notable moments

  • A remote presenter and muffled audio. Lauren Victor of the Wasatch Front Regional Council attended online after coming down with a cold, and warned early that the room came through "a little muffled when there's multiple people talking" — a caveat that shows up in the recording as well.
  • "It's both with 200,000 people." When Scanlon framed the choice as lakefront or Pioneer Crossing, a speaker rejected the either/or outright, citing the combined population of Eagle Mountain and Saratoga Springs as reason for both.
  • A correction on the central corridor. A speaker opened by saying they were "thrilled that the central corridor is under construction." Victor stepped in to clarify that the grey line on the map covers corridors under construction or with a locally preferred alternative, and that construction should not be assumed.
  • The land-cost warning. The most forceful contribution of the night was not a question but a statement: preserving the same corridors and alignments across all the cities matters now, because "the last thing we need to do is build a bunch more houses and businesses that are going to have to get torn down."
  • A missing FrontRunner connection. Scanlon volunteered that the map does not quite show what the partners agreed in September — a yellow line "should probably get to the American Fork FrontRunner Station."
