## What was discussed

### 2.A — Fiscal Year 2028 Budget Planning Kickoff: process and goals

This item opened planning for the budget year that starts July 2027. Kimberly called it a planning meeting. Staff had met each councilmember one-on-one, and they wanted the council to agree on the plan as a group before the internal staff kickoff on October 22.

Terrence said the council had asked for three things last cycle. The first was ways to measure services. The second was more and earlier budget work meetings. The third was using every city channel to reach residents. He set three goals for this cycle: agree on priorities, look five years ahead instead of one, and measure what the money achieves. He described the budget as the city's most important policy document. He noted that the Government Finance Officers Association now pushes cities to write budgets that ordinary readers can follow, and admitted "it's really hard to read 300, 400 pages of budget."

He then laid out roles. The council sets priorities and the level of service it wants. The mayor and city manager turn that into a recommended budget, due by law at the first meeting in May. Finance runs the process and analyzes requests. Departments justify and later manage their budgets.

### 2.A — The budget calendar

Councilmember Wright stopped the presentation to ask that a paper copy of the calendar be printed so members could mark it up. Terrence then walked through it. Staff hold workshops in November on service levels and five-year needs. Department requests are due December 3. December 8 brings a public safety session, with the Utah County Sheriff's Office ready to describe its level of service. The council discusses service levels on December 15 and holds the Budget Congress on January 27. Sessions on capital projects, revenue and staff pay follow, with the five-year capital improvement plan up for adoption on March 16. The tentative budget is due May 4 and adoption is set for June 15.

Councilmember Wright asked whether the internal documents behind each deadline could go to the council. "I've been here a number of years and I don't typically see those," he said. In his view, seeing the full size of the need might "compel" the council to drop things that are nice to have. Councilmember Clark agreed: seeing it would help members explain to residents why some items were cut. Terrence said the December 15 session was added for that purpose.

Clark asked staff to avoid the busiest May dates and the day before school starts, which residents had complained about last year. She also suggested a public version of the calendar tied to events like the farmers market. Councilmember Huish asked for a real "Budget in Brief," a 5- to 10-page handout for the open house. He said that last year he ended up screenshotting slides to understand the budget.

### 2.A — Longer sessions around the April conference

The mayor asked whether the council wanted to use its time at the April Utah League of Cities and Towns conference for extra sessions. Councilmember Wright said meeting on the travel day last year cost nothing beyond a hotel conference room, and that for him, "the more the better." Councilmember Whiting proposed folding the April 13 meeting into the same week. Terrence said that session covers one-time requests such as vehicles and equipment, and it could move. The mayor joked about riding down in a Corvette that the city would raffle off.

Kimberly asked whether sessions that don't need much staff should be held in the evening, as this 6 p.m. meeting was. Councilmember Wright said Councilmember Wood would prefer evenings. Clark and Whiting said they were flexible. Wright later noted that the conference website had not confirmed the April dates. Terrence said he had heard them at a meeting of the city managers' association.

### 2.A — Tax notice process and the fire service change

The mayor asked when the council would need to know whether a "truth in taxation" process applies. That is the state's public notice and hearing process for property tax changes. Kimberly said the decision must come before the tentative budget is adopted, because that is when the proposed rate is set. The old March deadline to notify the county no longer applies.

Kimberly said the question turns on fire service. If the city moves from one fire agency to another (the transcript renders them as "UFSA" and "UFA"), the old agency would stop charging its levy and the city would start charging one. She called it "a transition" and "not an increase in tax." Councilmember Wright described it as moving the levy from one bucket to another. Kimberly said the state process may still be required, and staff have asked the Tax Commission about it. If the process applies, the council would adopt an interim budget in June and hold the hearing and final adoption in August.

### 2.A — Staffing per resident

Councilmember Wright asked for a measure of how many employees each department has for the people it serves. He used his own example: about 1,200 building permits at 4.4 residents per home adds about 5,000 people. How many employees does it take to keep service at today's level? The mayor said a staff member, Natalie, has been continuing a study that interns started, comparing cities on figures like parks employees per acre. Ben said she is contacting partner cities and the League, and he gave no timeline. The mayor said he expected it "dang sure by March" and hoped for sooner.

### 2.A — A five-year forecast instead of a one-year view

Terrence said staff want to build a long-term model of revenue, spending, staffing and projects, similar to the recent utility rate study. Councilmember Wright asked how far ahead it would look. Brad said the city's impact fee facilities plans look beyond five or six years. State law limits only the window for collecting the fees.

Kimberly said staff aim to show two years in the preliminary budget and at least five years of revenue forecast. Wright pushed back. He said the council needs public discussion of tax revenue "we know are coming," including the municipal energy tax (MET) from large new users, whose timing can be estimated. He said such figures have been "very vague, even when we've asked for it." Kimberly said staff will do a five-year revenue forecast. She cautioned that a levy should match today's costs, not needs five years out.

The mayor put the problem this way: "we've spent year 2028 MET 15 times." He asked what the taxable value would be once the Meta expansion and the Williams project, due in 2029, come on the tax rolls. Kimberly said this is the first year the city has a full-time budget manager. She said nothing has been withheld; the operating-cost forecasts don't exist yet. Wright accepted that: "I don't feel like I have the information I need either." Huish asked for a simple five-year pro forma, a rough projection of revenue and costs, so big road projects aren't pushed aside for easy ones.

Brad warned that higher impact fees don't mean more money, because collections depend on how many homes get built. Clark asked that the document be called a "forecast" or "projection," not a "plan." Residents will hold a plan against the city, she said, but "the weather might change."

### 2.A — Budget Congress and the communication plan

Terrence said the January Budget Congress is a listening day. Departments will report what they achieved, what blocks them, their current service levels, and what would happen to service if a request were approved or denied. The communications department has made a budget web page and will use the newsletter, email and social media, with a short graphic after each work meeting.

He asked the council whether to mail residents the budget dates. Councilmember Whiting said online was enough. Clark said this year showed many residents aren't reached online, even with a 600-page budget published. She estimated a postcard would cost a couple thousand dollars. Huish preferred a strong Budget in Brief and a campaign pointing people to it, since a mailer to every home "would be pretty costly." Wright suggested a QR code on utility bills.

The mayor said residents ignored cottage meetings, web pages and park visits until the county's tax notice arrived. Kimberly said more than half of residents get paperless bills. She supported trying a postcard after the tentative budget, inviting people to the open house, and dropping it if engagement doesn't improve. Clark also suggested $400 to $500 of yard signs. Staff will price the options.
