**In short:** The council spent most of a long Tuesday work session reviewing early-year budget numbers and hearing results of a feasibility study on turning Eagle Mountain into a hub for "advanced air mobility" (drones, electric aircraft, and possibly a small airport). At the evening meeting, members approved several routine items, tabled a zoning change on power lines in agricultural land for more work, approved a farm-product home business rule, and dedicated a wildlife corridor to the city's late wildlife biologist, Todd Black.

**Present:** Mayor Jared Gray; Councilmembers Melissa Clark, Brett Wright, Rich Wood, Zac Huish. Councilmember Craig Whiting was excused, out of town on a family trip.

**Staff:** Terrence (finance director, budget report); Kimberly (finance/treasury staff); Marcus (city attorney); Brandon (community development director, agricultural zone ordinance); Stephen (planner, greenhouse/home-business ordinance); John (legislative affairs, fencing standards item); Abby (economic development, aviation hub project lead); Ben (staff, information items and aviation remarks); Brandon Larson (parks and recreation, introduced new staff); Justin Dodd (newly hired wildlife biologist); Steve Domino, Clint Harper (Future Flight Global), and Paul Dameron (Dameron Consulting) presented the airport feasibility study; Mare Burningham (co-founder, Eagle Mountain Nature and Wildlife Alliance) and Bailey Hedlund (Utah State University Associate Director of Advancement) presented the Todd Black scholarship; Mariah Black (Todd Black's daughter) and Paul Raymond (Utah National Guard/Camp Williams land manager) spoke in his honor; Alina Pringle, of West Desert Airpark, answered aviation questions; chamber of commerce staff answered questions on the ladder-sign agreement.

## What was discussed

### 1A — Quarterly budget report
Terrence, the city's finance director, gave the council its first-quarter financial update for the fiscal year that began July 1. Citywide, the city had collected about $29.5 million and spent about $18.8 million against a roughly $398 million budget, most of which is tied to wastewater treatment plant bond proceeds. Compared with the same period last year, revenue was up about $5.7 million, driven mostly by one-time sales tax activity and the city's first RAP tax (restaurant and pub) collections, about $450,000. Personnel costs rose about 12% due to compensation changes, while capital project spending remained low because construction season was ending.

Councilmember Huish asked for future reports to break out revenue by category, specifically residential versus commercial versus data-center property tax, and to separate the Municipal Energy Tax paid by large power users like data centers from the regular franchise tax bucket, so residents can see who is paying what. Terrence agreed to work on that breakdown, including for the February budget season. Huish also asked for a simple balance-sheet view showing how restricted fund balances, such as the roughly $3 million sitting from a past utility sale, might be put to use rather than left earning interest; Terrence said he would consult with the city's finance team on whether that is feasible to report quarterly.

### 1B — Airport/advanced air mobility feasibility study
Economic development staff member Abby introduced a team that had studied whether Eagle Mountain should build some kind of aviation hub, ranging from a small drone and electric-aircraft ("eVTOL") facility to a full runway capable of serving corporate jets. The consultants, Steve Domino and Clint Harper of Future Flight Global, argued the city has an unusual opportunity: a skilled tech workforce that commutes out of Eagle Mountain every day, open airspace not crowded by Salt Lake City or Provo air traffic, and a region already connected to markets like Las Vegas and St. George. They framed the project as a way to keep jobs, and the tax revenue and commute time that come with them, inside the city, while also warning that any decision made now will shape the city's character for the next 20 years.

Council reaction was cautious. Councilmember Wright said he did not want Eagle Mountain to become "like a Provo or a Heber" airport but was open to smaller-scale uses, especially drone and eVTOL technology, and asked what the real financial and practical tradeoffs would look like. Councilmember Clark agreed she has no interest in replicating Provo's airport, and wants the city to avoid becoming dependent on any single industry the way it was hurt when Tyson closed. Councilmember Huish said he doubted corporate jet demand was realistic for Eagle Mountain and favored focusing on drone research instead, while stressing that noise needs careful study. Councilmember Wood was the most skeptical, saying residents haven't been asked whether they even want this, that she dislikes the specific area being studied because it sits near large residential lots and a planned regional park, and that the city should not be "fronting the money" for a venture with uncertain payoff. Staff member Ben countered that drone cargo flights and military helicopter traffic are already happening over the city regardless of whether Eagle Mountain builds anything, comparing the technology's arrival to the rise of the automobile, and said the city has a chance to help shape it responsibly rather than watch it happen without any say. The city has scheduled a public open house for Monday, November 23, from 6 to 8 p.m. at City Hall to gather resident feedback; the mayor said he wants to see it more widely advertised before the council leans on it to help make a decision. Alina Pringle of the nearby West Desert Airpark, invited to comment, said she sees room for both a second airport and a vertiport focus in the region, and offered to partner with the city.

### 11A — Trailhead change order (Lake Mountain Corridor median removal)
This consent-agenda item, pulled for discussion at Councilmember Huish's request, concerned a $1.44 million change order to remove traffic medians from Trailhead Drive up to what staff clarified is called the Lake Mountain Corridor (not Lake Mountain Road, a naming point that caused some confusion during the discussion). The original signal-light project at that intersection came in under budget, and the council had wanted the leftover money used to remove the medians for safety, emergency access, future road widening, and the city's parade route. Staff disclosed that an additional $265,000 for steel traffic-signal mast arms, previously purchased through the Utah Department of Transportation but now bought separately, had been left out of the original estimate and will come back as a separate budget amendment. Staff clarified that the remaining stretch of road to Airport Road will be handled by the Eagle's Landing developer, not the city. After the clarification, Councilmember Wright said his question was answered and the item stayed on the consent agenda.

### 14A/14B — Todd Black Memorial Scholarship and Wildlife Corridor
The council honored Todd Black, the city's former wildlife biologist and open space manager who died earlier this year. Mare Burningham, co-founder of the Eagle Mountain Nature and Wildlife Alliance, described how a chance connection led to partnering with Utah State University, where Bailey Hedlund, the university's associate director of advancement, introduced the new Todd A. Black Wildlife Endowed Scholarship. The goal is to raise $250,000 to fund five $2,000 awards per semester in perpetuity, with an expendable fund available sooner while the endowment builds. Todd's daughter, Mariah Black, read a tribute to her father's career in wildlife biology and land management, and Paul Raymond, who manages land for the Utah National Guard near Camp Williams, described working with Todd to build a wildlife migration fence and conservation easements around the base. The council then unanimously passed a resolution formally renaming the city's wildlife migration corridor the Todd A. Black Memorial Wildlife Corridor.

### 15A — Valley Crossroads Chamber of Commerce ladder-sign extension
The council considered a 3-year extension of its 2023 agreement letting the chamber manage "ladder sign" advertising structures in city rights-of-way. Councilmember Clark recused herself because she is employed by the chamber. Councilmember Wood asked how much revenue the arrangement generates, whether the city tracks compliance with sign rules, and whether the signs are maintained properly; staff and city attorney Marcus said the city doesn't track revenue since the signs stopped being a city revenue source under the agreement, and that compliance is handled on a complaint basis rather than active monitoring. A chamber representative said data shows local small businesses occupy the majority of sign slots, with developers and outside businesses holding fewer. Wood also noted the agreement helps the city control what kind of advertising appears in public rights-of-way, preventing objectionable groups from claiming the same space as free speech. The council approved the extension 3-0.

### 16A — Agricultural zone ordinance (public hearing, tabled)
Community Development Director Brandon explained that the agricultural zone currently bars utilities, including ordinary water, sewer, and power lines, after a 2024 code change meant to stop large transmission infrastructure from being built there. The problem: Rocky Mountain Power needs to run a transmission line from Spanish Fork to the Mercer substation serving the Meta data center, and that route crosses agricultural land. After exploring and rejecting an overlay-zone approach used by Spanish Fork, staff proposed restoring ordinary utilities as a permitted use and adding transmission lines as a special use, which comes with additional landscaping and siting standards. Councilmember Wood raised concern that the broad term "utility facilities" could be misread decades from now as covering power generation facilities, something the council has separately discussed restricting; she asked for a clarifying note tying any special use explicitly to transmission structures and excluding power generation. A resident asked in public comment whether the change would also apply to things like wind farms, churches, or UTA bus facilities; staff and the city attorney said those are governed by separate code sections and are not affected. The council voted 4-0 to table the ordinance to December 1, with the public hearing left open, so staff can add Wood's clarifying language.

### 16B — Greenhouse and farm product home business ordinance (public hearing)
Planner Stephen presented a change allowing home-based businesses to sell farm, greenhouse, and floral products grown or produced on the same property, while keeping existing limits on home-business hours, employee counts, and customer traffic unchanged. The ordinance keeps retail resale of goods not produced on-site prohibited, and bars on-site animal slaughtering except for home consumption permitted under the state's Home Consumption and Homemade Food Act, which staff said effectively covers only chickens and rabbits. Councilmember Huish and Councilmember Clark discussed the state law's scope. In public comment, a resident raised unrelated concerns about data centers and future road widening near agricultural land; the mayor clarified that data centers require a separate industrial zoning overlay and are not permitted under the agricultural zone being amended here. The council approved the ordinance 4-0.

## Notable moments

- **A resident's costly lesson in code enforcement.** A resident described coming home from an out-of-town trip to find two moving trailers towed 25 miles away and a $1,400 bill, and asked the city to consider giving residents more notice before towing. Councilmember Huish said the story didn't match "the Eagle Mountain way" and asked to review the underlying towing policy at a future work session.
- **A charity invitation from the schools.** Charlotte Dukos, the student council advisor at Cedar Valley High School, invited the community to "Christmas in the Valley" on December 4, a fundraiser with a free clothing and toy swap benefiting local families.
- **Councilmember Wood addressed online criticism directly.** Describing the city as feeling "like a family that's had a quarrel" over this year's tax increase, Wood said claims circulating on social media about the council's integrity were "absolute lies" and asked residents to seek common ground heading into Thanksgiving.
- **An emotional scholarship ceremony.** Todd Black's daughter, Mariah, nearly in tears, thanked the city for giving her father, a wildlife biologist, the chance to pursue his passion; Councilmember Huish shared a personal story about his own daughter's illness and a city-organized celebration years earlier to explain why the moment mattered to him.
- **Veterans Day reflections.** The mayor described a 15-year tradition of taking a disabled veteran on an elk hunt, aired this year on a local outdoors broadcast.
- **FDR and the automobile as an aviation analogy.** Staff member Ben compared public wariness of early aviation technology to early resistance to the automobile, arguing the city has a chance to shape the coming air mobility industry responsibly rather than be overrun by it.
- **A dare to go listen first.** Councilmember Wood urged fellow council members to sit for an hour at the Heber City Airport on a Saturday before forming an opinion on aircraft noise, calling it essential before any siting decision.
