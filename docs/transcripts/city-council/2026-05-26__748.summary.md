## 2. Per-Item Enrichment

### 2.A — Property Tax Impact Schedule

This item exists because Eagle Mountain is in the state truth-in-taxation process, and Kimberly explained that any meeting touching the FY2027 proposed budget legally must include a reading of the property-tax impact schedule; that is why it recurs on this and the upcoming June 16 agenda. The Mayor read the notice into the record with no action taken. As read, the city is considering a rate increase to generate roughly **$6,958,141** in additional revenue, moving from a current rate of 0.000534 and current property-tax revenue of about $2,839,998 to proposed revenue near $9,798,139 — a stated **245% increase** in the city's property-tax revenue. The Mayor tied the "affected budget" explicitly to the Utah County Sheriff's Office contract (Eagle Mountain Division), noting the funds would support core public-safety operations and added deputies to keep pace with growth. His delivery of the per-household impact figures was visibly garbled (see §5).

### 2.B — Budget Discussion: Process Overview

Councilmember Clark opened by asking staff to walk residents through how the city actually vets spending, specifically to answer the recurring public question of "why don't you just cut things?" City Manager Ben responded that the FY2027 process began internally in October with department-by-department meetings, a "five-year needs assessment," and a "personnel justification" step in which departments must explain what service degradation would follow if a position is *not* funded. A committee of Ben, Kimberly, Terrence, and the Mayor then scores requests against the city's strategic objectives and available funding sources. Kimberly and others stressed a philosophy they said distinguishes Eagle Mountain: departments budget from "what does it take to do next year exactly what we're doing this year," and only add if new revenue exists. Several officials emphasized that the budget committee "has never, ever just cut a position" — reductions instead come from department heads volunteering to "get by" with fewer people.

### 2.B — Events Budget, Nolan Park, and Capital Set-Asides

The Mayor used the events budget to make a larger point about staff frugality, noting the events department returned about **$30,000** relative to last year (partly by dropping the "techno blast"), and asked whether council wanted to leave a cushion to expand smaller events like the Easter egg hunt. He also reported that the **$750,000 already allocated for Nolan Park** from last year went unspent and is "depreciating" while the council debates the park's future design, and flagged the north service area (a salt shed / lean-to on land the city already owns) as another opportunity funded partly by a streets department that came in under budget. Councilmembers Wright and Clark both endorsed appropriating the $30,000 to events, and Wright and Whiting supported the salt-storage idea to save fuel and plow time.

### 2.B — Library Staffing

Councilmember Whiting pressed staff on why a library position that appeared on an earlier list from Terrence was absent from the tentative-budget list Kimberly re-sent. He relayed his understanding that the library is understaffed "by something like eight or nine employees" amid growing subscriptions. Staff (Ben, with Library Director Michelle available online) reframed the problem as primarily one of **square footage rather than headcount**, arguing the more appropriate time to staff up is when a new city hall and library are planned and built. Kimberly clarified that only "a handful" of requested positions did not survive, and that P2 positions were flagged "on hold" for funding reasons rather than being judged unnecessary.

### 2.B — Observatory and Sweetwater Park

Councilmember Wright advocated programming impact fees toward a new park in the Sweetwater/city-center area, comparing it to the Smith Ranch Park investment. Separately, the Mayor and Natalie reported the observatory project (roughly $1.5 million, much of it donated) recently secured a **$250,000 county transportation award**, leaving it about $250,000 short; the Mayor said he would pursue a grant to close the gap. When Whiting asked about long-term maintenance and operations, Natalie said costs would be "very minimal" because it is a small standalone structure near an already-maintained park restroom, with the Utah Valley University experts and the local astronomy club (affectionately dubbed "the nerds") handling the telescopes and programming.

### 2.B — School-Zone Speed Tables

Councilmember Wright, who said he has worked on this with Councilmember Wood, made an extended push to fund traffic-calming speed tables at elementary schools (naming Silver Lake, Brookhaven, Mountain Trails, and Desert Sky), framing it as a child-safety issue "far past time" to address. David (engineering) put concrete retrofits at roughly $20,000–$30,000 each (asphalt $15,000–$17,000) and estimated four or five locations near $200,000; Wright suggested a $100,000 line and possibly using utility-cell money as one-time funds. David cautioned that the city should first adopt **code governing where speed tables are allowed** to avoid "opening the floodgates," and noted impacts on plowing and emergency response. Clark asked about the wheel-cutout design used in Salt Lake to preserve emergency-vehicle access, and Whiting endorsed the concept, citing a $7,000 speed bump at his Lehi workplace that solved a speeding problem.

### 2.B — Personnel Requests (the P1/P2 List)

Much of the meeting was a working review of roughly 28 requested positions totaling about **$2.6 million** (of which about $1.3 million hits the general fund; the balance is covered by permit, development, and utility fees). Councilmember Huish (online) counseled fiscal restraint — working with the first ~$1 million tranche rather than the full ~$1.8 million, invoking the "if-come" principle and warning specifically against using RAP tax dollars to fund ongoing salaries. Councilmembers Wright and Clark repeatedly framed the tension as "boots on the ground" (parks/open-space technicians, crew leads doing physical work) versus higher-cost leadership roles. After extended discussion, the council reached consensus to advance the full list **except** a handful flagged for further scrutiny: the assistant city attorney, purchasing agent, payroll/benefits administrator, community services director, and (briefly) the wildlife biologist and arborist technician. The wildlife biologist — which Huish thought he was championing but was already in the tentative budget as a P1 — retained support.

### 2.B — Community Services Director (held)

This ~$147,000 functional-area director role drew the sharpest debate. Ben argued a good community services director would "pay for themselves" by building soccer, senior, recreation, and events programming and serving as staff liaison to the community services board, and staff stressed that starting a new service area requires leadership first, not last. Kimberly (from administrative services) reinforced that this is a director-level, not middle-management, position. Councilmember Clark countered that the city should first fund and retain the "boots on the ground" already delivering events, rec, library, and senior services, and questioned a salary set **$27,000 above the city attorney's**; she and others also noted a hire could not start until roughly August/September, missing the summer season where the payoff would show. The council consensus was to **wait**, with Steve confirming he can continue absorbing the duties (chiefly at the expense of his administrative support to Ben).

### 2.B — Assistant City Attorney (pending data)

City Attorney Marcus explained that heavy current outside-counsel spending stems from a spike in litigation, and that in-house counsel with litigation experience plus a paralegal would be far cheaper than paying roughly $400/hour to outside firms. He cited responsibilities falling by the wayside — attending DRC weekly, faster ordinance review, a stalled Comcast franchise renewal. Councilmember Clark initially favored outsourcing; the Mayor and Wright leaned toward supporting the full-time position. The council left it **pinned pending data**: staff committed to report outside-counsel spending, which the Mayor later stated would total close to **$150,000** by June 30. Whiting closed the exchange by joking about whether funding the position would finally get the city its justice court, to which Marcus replied the city is "legally entitled to one."

### 2.B — Purchasing Agent and Payroll/Benefits Administrator (advanced)

Both positions advanced after direct workload testimony. Purchasing agent Melissa Yates, becoming emotional, described handling every purchase over $75,000, every contract, all change orders, and roughly 10 RFPs since late March while working overtime weekly. HR's Angela testified the city has grown to **236 employees**, hiring 115 in the last 12 months (63 year-to-date), and that a recent IRS audit flagged payroll items currently handled by only a part-time person; she asked for a full-time, payroll-law-certified role. The council marked both to advance.

### 2.B — Arborist Technician (advanced)

Initially question-marked, this P2 was clarified as a third member for the city's tree crew (one community forester plus one existing arborist technician). The Mayor reframed it as a "boots on the ground" role — the person who would remove the tree stumps and trim sight-blocking trees near school signs that councilmembers had complained about — and the council agreed to advance it.

### 2.B — Lone Tree Egress Road

The longest single debate concerned emergency access for the Lone Tree subdivision. David priced a permanent ~5,100-foot collector connection to Arnett Peak/Airport Road at roughly **$5 million** (ribbon road, excluding utilities), while staff (Zach Hilton, David) proposed a near-term fix using asphalt millings from the imminent Eagle Mountain Boulevard mill-and-overlay for dust control on the existing dirt path. Councilmembers Wright and Clark pushed to separate the two: pursue the *permanent* egress by securing easements and alignment with landowners (Perry, JDH, with Ivory having agreed to accelerate its southern segment) while treating the millings as only a temporary phase-one measure. Councilmember Whiting drew a firm line that land west of Lone Tree remains **agricultural-zoned** and that developers floating residential concepts "really need to come talk to us first." The council's takeaway, per the Mayor, was to build a concrete **timeline** (with Ben's help), add Lone Tree to the "listening tour," and stage the work — temporary millings now, easement/alignment work next — rather than commit $5 million of taxpayer funds to a road development would eventually build anyway.

### 2.B — Property-Tax Rate Decision Timeline

Near the end, the Mayor asked whether the council was prepared to endorse the 245% figure. Councilmembers Wright and Clark declined to commit, saying they want the public hearings first while acknowledging they are "considering it." Kimberly clarified the binding step: the council must set the **proposed** rate that appears on tax statements and notices at the **June 16** meeting (to certify by June 22), alongside the interim budget — though the final adoption number can still change.
