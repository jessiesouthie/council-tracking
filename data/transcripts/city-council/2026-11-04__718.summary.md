Council members gave staff direction on all the work-session topics without voting, then unanimously passed a rezoning, an artificial-turf ordinance, a land-sale process, and the consent agenda. **Settled without a vote:**
- On the business-incentive proposal, the council told Economic Development Director Abby Ivory not to publish a formal incentive framework; instead, members want to steer development toward target industries through the general plan and zoning tools like the RTI overlay, so the city doesn't "show its cards" to site selectors.

- The Mayor will circulate the current board and liaison assignment list by email ahead of the usual January reshuffle, for council members to flag preferences.
- John Magnus was asked to bring back a proposal for standardized decorative fencing along arterial roads at a future meeting.

## What was discussed

### 1.A — Strategic Industry Performance Incentive Program
Economic Development Director Abby Ivory pitched a formal scoring system for offering property-tax rebates to companies in advanced manufacturing, energy, life sciences, and aerospace, explicitly excluding retail and data centers. She framed it as a way to diversify the city's job base beyond tech, since many residents commute to Silicon Slopes and are exposed to layoffs there. Councilmember Craig Whiting questioned the entire approach, arguing that publishing a framework shows site selectors the city's "cards" and weakens its negotiating position. Councilmember Brett Wright agreed, saying he's unwilling to "telegraph" the city's limits, and suggested the better tool is using the general plan and an expanded RTI overlay zone to designate target areas without naming dollar incentives.

Mayor Gray tried to find middle ground, saying the goal isn't to hide everything from Ivory but to give her clear guardrails, such as a 20-year cap on incentive terms, so she can rule out bad-fit prospects at conferences. Councilmember Melissa Clark pushed to identify what the city's "next data center" industry should be before committing to any program, and raised the needs of smaller, local businesses that can't access incentives built for massive capital projects. Ivory agreed to keep working the conference circuit and offered to explore a revolving loan fund for smaller businesses, funded through administrative fees the city already collects.

### 1.B — Water Rights Code
City staff member Brandon walked the council through a new water rights chapter meant to fix inconsistent past practice, including creating a "water rights analyst" position, requiring a will-serve letter at the concept-plan stage, and setting a 2006 cutoff for the water-right priority dates the city will accept. The core debate centered on when developers should have to hand over water rights. Developer Scott Hazard argued that requiring full dedication at the master development plan stage, years before a project sells out, forces him to tie up water and capital he could otherwise deploy where the market is ready, and that the private sector normally proves financial readiness at final plat, not master plan.

City Attorney Marcus countered that the current, looser system is why about 20,000 vested units in the city lack the water to support them, comparing it to abandoned half-built projects he saw on a mission in the former Soviet Union. Councilmember Wright worried aloud that letting a developer "demonstrate" water without dedicating it could let the same water rights get claimed for more than one project, noting the city was burned once already when 40 acre-feet were dedicated twice. Wright proposed a compromise: let developers bank water into a city-owned well, where they'd still own it but couldn't pull it back out, as a middle step between showing water and formally dedicating it. Staff and Hazard agreed to keep refining the details outside the meeting.

### 1.C — Deer Run Concept Plan
Planner Robert Hobbs and Ivory Homes representative Brad presented a revised concept plan for the Deer Run property near the Airport Road extension. Two parcels the developer doesn't own were pulled from the plan per earlier council direction, and a large wash running through the site was confirmed not to be part of the protected wildlife corridor, though it may still fall under the city's 75-foot structure setback for natural washes. Marcus and Brad disagreed on whether that code applies outside a designated "sensitive lands" zone; both sides agreed to work it out in the development agreement and suggested the city map its natural washes going forward to avoid the same confusion.

Councilmember Whiting said he'd like to see the city sell its two adjacent parcels to the developer rather than leave them stranded, and Mayor Gray and Hobbs discussed reshaping some large lots in the northwest corner into an equestrian-style enclave. Brad asked that the council let water dedication happen at final plat rather than the master plan stage, noting that Ivory Homes already over-dedicates because it builds water-wise landscaping. Marcus confirmed an active master development plan application exists, so the project moves next toward a draft development agreement.

### 1.D — Harmony Phase A Design Exception
Planner Robert Hobbs explained that a 3.63-acre parcel in the Harmony development, originally platted for 90 condo units, is caught by a code change the city passed after an earlier Harmony phase was built entirely on private service drives instead of public streets. Flagship Homes, represented by Luke McComb, now wants to build 68 townhomes instead, accessed by "Lulu Lane," which functions as an overbuilt parking aisle rather than a street. Because sewer and water lines are already installed on both sides of that aisle, converting it to a full public street would be costly, but code says townhomes must front a street, not a service drive.

Councilmember Wright, who visited the site personally, argued the real problem is that the units would front an unbuilt Walden Parkway behind a required masonry wall, with no sidewalk for trick-or-treaters or residents. He and Councilmember Whiting converged on flipping the townhomes to face inward toward Lulu Lane, pushing the units toward a rear service drive, and keeping a continuous wall along Walden Parkway instead of an awkward mix of fencing. Public Works staff noted garages can't front directly on Lulu Lane if it's classified as a collector road, which adds complexity. The council didn't resolve the design but asked staff, the applicant, and the project engineer to meet and bring back a revised plan, with Wright insisting Lulu Lane must ultimately be HOA-maintained and that the city will need utility easements regardless of the final layout.

### 1.E — Wildlife Corridor Naming
Staff asked the council to settle on a name before drafting a resolution for next week. The council agreed on "Todd A. Black Memorial Wildlife Corridor," keeping "Memorial" in the name after a brief back-and-forth about whether it was redundant. Members left open whether a future wildlife bridge over the corridor should get its own separate marker or share the same name, deciding that question can wait until the bridge is actually built.

### City announcements and Citizens Academy
City Manager Ben Reeves ran through a long list of updates: Eagle Mountain was named a Healthy Utah Community, the city is pursuing an aviation and advanced air mobility hub near Camp Williams, and a new AI chatbot named "Emmy" is live on the city website and by text to answer resident questions in more than 70 languages. He also flagged the city's 30th anniversary treasure hunt, the library's toy drive, Thanksgiving basket nominations for seniors, the leaf-bag program, and the Veterans Day breakfast on November 14. Separately, the city's Citizens Academy graduated twelve residents who completed a six-week course touring city departments, including the wastewater treatment plant.

### Public comment — Lake Mountain Road
A resident who bought a farm on Lake Mountain Road said the dirt road has become "a lake mountain," with deep mud, a buried water line hazard, and worsening ruts she attributes partly to construction traffic for a nearby data center. She also said a sofa and tires she reported through the city's service app were marked resolved without being picked up. Councilmember Huish followed up, saying he's had a similar experience with a tree-stump removal request being closed before the work was finished, and suggested the city adopt a "double do" verification step before closing service tickets. Mayor Gray and Marcus noted the city likely can't force the adjacent charter school, John Hancock Academy, to finish paving the road past its own entrance, since state law limits what cities can require of schools.

### Council and Mayor reports
Council members used their report time to cover a mix of board updates and personal notes: Clark on the American Legion-run Veterans Day breakfast, Wright on the Development Review Committee and senior center Thanksgiving meal program, Whiting on the Youth Council's Halloween party and a question about whether the UCCU Express approval had incorporated Planning Commission conditions, and Huish on the Community Services Board and the city's podcast of public meetings. Mayor Gray mentioned meeting security expert Frank Abagnale at a league of cities conference and disclosed he has been working, without a finished plan yet, on a commuter bus and park-and-ride system for data center workers to ease regional traffic.

### 13.A — Ranches Golf Course Agreement Addendum
City Attorney Marcus explained that the city is widening Ranches Parkway and needs about 3.5 acres of right-of-way from the Ranches Golf Course. Rather than a cash purchase, the golf course's owner will give the city that land to settle outstanding debts, including unpaid amounts tied to the original sale of the course decades ago and unpaid utility bills. Councilmember Clark confirmed the project is funded through the Mountainland Association of Governments, so the city's related construction costs, such as relocating cart paths, will ultimately be reimbursed.

### 14.A — Land Disposal Application (Mark Baum)
City Recorder Lacie Messerly presented a request from Mark Baum, who built a concrete block wall on a strip of city-owned land behind his house without realizing it wasn't his property, after years of drainage problems eroding a nearby pathway. Baum asked to either buy the strip, donate the wall to the city, or remove it and get help with the water problem instead. Council members, after confirming the city would have almost no practical way to access or maintain that sliver of land, favored letting Baum buy it and keep the wall, both to limit the city's liability for the structure and because the steep drop-off makes the land otherwise unusable to the city.

### 15.A — Oak Hollow and Parkway Crossroads Rezoning
Planner Mickie Mills presented a cleanup rezoning that assigns current, codified zoning designations to the Oak Hollow and Parkway Crossroads areas, replacing an old "Village Core Commercial" designation that had been too vague to administer. Staff said the change doesn't add density or traffic, but does make it easier to approve ordinary commercial uses, citing a past business license application at Ranches and Pony Express that stalled under the old zoning. Councilmember Clark asked whether the switch would eliminate any uses the old zone allowed, such as mixed-use buildings; staff said any such request would now go through a development agreement instead. No one spoke at the public hearing.

### 15.B — Artificial Turf Ordinance
Senior Planner David Stroud presented new standards for artificial turf, drafted with input from turf installers, recommending a roughly 1.5-inch pile depth and organic infill materials like sand or cork over inorganic crumb rubber. The amendment also removes a rear-yard landscaping rule the city isn't allowed to enforce under state law. Council members asked about utility conflicts, since turf would sit over gas, water, and electrical lines in the front-yard utility easement, and confirmed property owners still bear the cost and responsibility if utility work requires digging up their turf. Front yards must still keep at least 50% live plant coverage. No one spoke at the public hearing.

### 16.A — Legislative Priorities Update
Legislative liaison John Magnus asked the council whether it still wants a standardized decorative fence design along arterial roads, noting a custom design had proven too costly. Mayor Gray asked to defer the question to a future meeting so Councilmember Wood, who had worked on the idea, can weigh in.

## Notable moments
- **A resident's flooded dirt road.** A woman who bought a farm on Lake Mountain Road described her street as "literally a lake mountain," with foot-deep water and a buried utility hazard made worse, she believes, by data center construction traffic.
- **The "double do" fix.** Councilmember Huish proposed the city stop closing resident service requests until a second person confirms the work was actually finished, after his own stump-removal complaint was marked resolved when it wasn't.
- **A poker metaphor steers policy.** Councilmember Whiting's objection that a published incentive framework would "show our cards" to site selectors reshaped the entire business-incentive discussion, pulling the council away from Abby Ivory's original proposal.
- **A site visit changes the debate.** Councilmember Wright's personal drive-through of the Harmony townhome site, where he found existing pavement and utilities already installed, drove the push to flip the units and rework Lulu Lane rather than simply deny the design exception.
- **A Soviet Union analogy.** City Attorney Marcus compared unfinished, water-less developments in Eagle Mountain to abandoned half-built construction he saw while serving a mission in the former Soviet Union, to explain why the city wants water rights locked down earlier in the approval process.
- **An accidental encroachment.** Resident Mark Baum built a concrete wall on city property without realizing it, and the council leaned toward selling him the land rather than making him tear it down.
- **Family comes first.** Councilmember Whiting left the dais mid-meeting for his daughter's senior-night soccer awards; Mayor Gray publicly congratulated her when Whiting returned.
