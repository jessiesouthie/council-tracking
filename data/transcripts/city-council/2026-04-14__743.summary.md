## 2. Per-Item Enrichment

### 2.A — Utah County Sheriff's Office staffing request

This was a budget-season request from the Utah County Sheriff's Office, which polices Eagle Mountain under contract, for seven additional sworn positions at an estimated additional cost of about $1.44 million. The chief deputy who runs the Eagle Mountain Division opened by insisting on the framing: "We are your police department," he said, noting that the Sheriff's Office has served the city since its inception, that deputies are assigned to Eagle Mountain rather than dispatched into it from elsewhere, and that they work out of city office space. The division currently fields 35 sworn officers — a chief deputy, a lieutenant, an officer specialist, five sergeants, four patrol teams of four deputies each, two animal control deputies, two traffic deputies, four detectives, a victim advocate, and coordinators running programs such as RAD and the volunteers-in-patrol.

The heart of the presentation was a workload study the chief deputy said he undertook at the council's own request from a year earlier, when the body pushed back on being given only an officers-per-thousand figure. Eagle Mountain now sits at 0.45 sworn officers per 1,000 residents, or roughly one per 2,200 people across about 77,000 residents and 50.3 square miles. He offered neighbors for comparison — Lehi at about 0.7 (one per 1,429), Saratoga Springs and Bluffdale at roughly one per 1,000 combined — while explicitly declining to argue that Eagle Mountain needs those ratios. What he leaned on instead were two ICMA benchmarks: the "Rule of 60," under which about 60 percent of sworn staff should be assigned to patrol (Eagle Mountain is at 68.5 percent, meaning it is heavy on patrol and thin on administration, supervision, and investigations), and the "saturation index," the share of available officer time consumed by calls. ICMA treats 50 to 60 percent as properly staffed and anything sustained above 60 percent as understaffed; Eagle Mountain runs 65 to 70 percent on average, and higher during afternoon and weekend peaks. Calls for service reached 25,699 last year, an 18.6 percent increase over the prior year — about 70 incidents a day, averaging 32 minutes each, consuming 38 officer-hours daily. Those figures, he stressed, exclude backup units, report time, and travel time, because he chose not to guess at data he did not have.

Asked by Councilmember Wood whether the call increase reflected growth or crime, the chief deputy was emphatic that it was population: "Eagle Mountain is not West Valley City. We don't have that crime rate. We are one of the safest cities in the state of Utah." Councilmember Clark asked whether community policing could address the specific complaints the council hears from the dais — she offered e-bikes as an example — and drew out what became the request's most concrete justification. Two dedicated community-oriented policing officers, he argued, could work with staff to get city ordinances onto the county's Master Offense Table so they can actually be charged in justice court, and could pursue permanent fixes for neighborhood traffic complaints alongside city traffic engineers rather than treating them with what he called the "temporary Band-Aid" of a traffic unit. Patrol deputies, on rotating twelve-hour shifts that flip between days and graveyards every two months, cannot sustain that kind of long-term relationship work. The formal ask is six deputies — one added to each of the four patrol teams, plus the two community officers — and one sergeant, to relieve Sergeant Max Morgan, whose span of control the chief deputy called "beyond best practices." The workload study, he conceded, actually calls for 8 to 10 additional patrol deputies and 11 to 13 additional sworn overall; he trimmed the request out of deference to the city's finances. Seven positions would move the saturation index to 55 to 60 percent and the ratio to 0.55 per 1,000 — still low, he said, but enough that "our guys can breathe a little bit."

He closed with a cost breakdown intended to correct what Councilmember Clark later called "some really bad misinformation" circulating publicly. The average deputy's salary and benefits run about $140,000; the fully loaded contract cost per deputy, including motor pool recapitalization, radios, radar, body and in-car video, HR, evidence and crime lab, records, the Spillman reporting system, roughly $1,100 a year in state-required liability insurance, and shared access to SWAT, search-and-rescue, canine, and drone resources, comes to about $200,000. A sergeant runs about $240,000 to $247,000. He also presented, at Clark's earlier request, a mapping-grid analysis of the area around the new Walmart: police incidents in that grid rose from roughly 62 in the first quarter of 2025 to 108 in the quarter Walmart opened (August 27) and 141 in the fourth quarter, a 73 percent increase year over year into the first quarter of 2026. He was careful to say this reflects traffic and volume, not that "things are going downhill." His summary line, repeated several times: "We're lean, and in this case, lean means fragile. This is a need. This isn't a want."

### 2.A — Council discussion: revenue, property tax, and how to fund public safety

What followed was the most consequential exchange of the afternoon, and it was not really about deputies — it was about the structural condition of the city's general fund. Councilmember Wright, in his fifth budget season, described the pattern: every year the Sheriff's Office asks, every year the council tries to accommodate within constraints, and every year the gap widens. He recalled a previous cycle in which the council funded two more deputies than then-Chief McDowell had requested. "The problem comes from the funding mechanism is not keeping up with the demand," he said. Residents object that new rooftops should pay for new service, "and they are, but they're paying for it at a rate that's 20 years old." He asked each of his colleagues to consider a specific question — what would it take to fund 8 to 10 rather than 7? — while naming the hard part out loud: roughly $600,000 in cuts to add three more, and an ongoing obligation once added. "If we're not providing adequate policing services, why are we sitting here?"

Councilmember Wood went further and made the sharpest remarks of the meeting. "I'm disappointed that we have not built this into our system, frankly," he said, adding that in three budget seasons "it seems like public safety is an afterthought every time." He argued the city is 13 sworn officers short of where it ought to be, that 8 to 10 is "not even an excess, it's just to get it to baseline," and that the answer is not another one-year accommodation but a standing policy tying public-safety staffing to population growth, with a tax mechanism to fund it. He connected the problem to the city's earlier water and sewer rate reckonings — "we're acting like we can just keep band-aiding this" — and reserved real anger for elected officials who campaign on never raising taxes: "All you've done is make it so that we can't pay for anything." Councilmember Huish, a nearly twenty-year resident, supplied the arithmetic behind the frustration: his property tax bill from the city is unchanged from a decade ago while the cost per deputy covering his neighborhood has risen, so a coverage ratio that once stood near 0.7 has eroded to 0.45.

Staff confirmed the diagnosis. The city administrator said flatly that "we don't have an expense problem, we have a revenue problem," noting that Utah's property tax has no cost-of-living escalator, that years of running lean have left little to cut, and that funding a core service out of sales tax exposes it to the volatility of retail. He pointed to Saratoga Springs, which historically covered 57 or 58 percent of public safety from property taxes and is working to get back to that level — the balance, he noted pointedly, being covered by sales tax revenue that partly consists of Eagle Mountain's own roughly 70 percent retail leakage into Saratoga Springs and Lehi. Two practical constraints were established for the record. First, Utah law does not permit a split rate: Councilmember Huish and Councilmember Wright walked through the mechanics, with the Mayor summarizing that a levy increase applies identically to commercial and residential parcels, with a 45 percent reduction applied to owner-occupied primary residences only — so a dollar of new levy costs a business a dollar and a homeowner 55 cents. The city administrator added that the city's data center agreements contractually bar changing property tax on those parcels alone in any case. Second, a change in state code this year means the city cannot spend any new property tax revenue until 30 days after the August truth-in-taxation hearing — which is to say, not before September regardless of what the council decides.

The Mayor offered the only concrete funding structure of the day, drawing on a phasing approach he and Councilmember Wright had used with Chief McDowell: hire four patrol deputies as revenue arrives (realistically September, not July) to bring every team to five, then add a second group of four at mid-year in January, which costs only half a year of salary in the first budget while establishing the ongoing expense. "Four and four gets us eight," he said, suggesting the sergeant and community officers could wait until January because immediate patrol coverage is the first concern. Councilmember Clark endorsed the 8-to-10 target on the grounds that deferring only guarantees a harder decision in a less certain economy, and attached two conditions she wanted remembered: getting Eagle Mountain's ordinances onto the Master Offense List so they can be enforced, and finally moving on a city justice court after more than 18 months of discussion. No decision was made; the city administrator twice deferred the "how" to the next budget meeting.

### 2.A — Transportation and the five-year capital improvement plan

The second half of the work session was the city engineer's first presentation of a budget cycle, covering the fiscal-year 2027 slate of transportation projects built off the impact fee facilities plan and the Transportation Master Plan. He described a project-delivery change he and capital project accountant Chris Waldron adopted: fund design a year ahead of construction, so the following year's construction request rests on an engineer's estimate rather than the deliberately inflated UDOT-template numbers used for long-range planning. That sequencing has both saved money — over $1.5 million on the current construction season — and exposed a project that had been badly underestimated.

The largest single item is the Ranches Parkway median removal, submitted to the Mountainland Association of Governments (MAG) two weeks earlier, with a presentation set for April 27 and an award decision expected May 4. The corridor carries about 33,000 vehicles per day against a capacity the engineer put in the neighborhood of 33,600 to 33,800 — "just a shy away from going over capacity." Because the existing medians run 36 to 38 feet wide and the corridor holds 202 feet of preserved right-of-way ("big props to past city folks who did that"), removing the medians yields two additional travel lanes and a center turn lane without acquisition, adding roughly 18,000 to 20,000 vehicles per day of capacity and giving emergency responders mid-block turnarounds. The engineer's larger strategic proposal was to complete Ranches Parkway and widen Pony Express from Mid Valley Road to the Saratoga Springs border — coordinating with Saratoga, which he said has both the right-of-way and the willingness — *before* the SR-73 highway reconstruction begins. That project is expected to be programmed in 2029 with construction in 2030, and he warned that its detoured traffic will land on corridors already over capacity. Since MAG money for Ranches would not arrive until fiscal 2029–30, he floated bonding or another alternate revenue source to start now. The Mayor, who sits on MAG, endorsed fronting costs and seeking reimbursement — noting he "fights with MAG all the time" over the unfairness of a system that rewards cities which wait and then amend for higher costs over cities like Eagle Mountain that prepay and save money. He also issued a sharp procedural warning: any project built before it is approved into the impact fee facilities plan and before MAG award is permanently ineligible for both. "We need to get those in now before we get in too big a hurry."

Individual projects drew detailed, and at times skeptical, engagement. At SR-73 and Campus Drive, a high-T continuous-green signal will let southbound traffic flow uninterrupted while a red phase halts northbound traffic so vehicles can egress into an acceleration lane; the scope grew to dual right-turn lanes after Walmart's traffic impacts appeared. Mayor Gray pressed hard on the geometry, arguing that only one lane is a true through lane and asking for both a deceleration right-turn lane and a deeper left-turn pocket; the engineer defended the model, explained that the left-turn pocket begins at the intersection and releases in a way the queuing analysis accommodates, and said space is simply too tight — "I wish we had more space." Councilmember Wood extracted the practical consequence that left egress east out of the Maverik will be closed by a concrete median, with drivers rerouted through the Stonehenge signal, and asked that residents and businesses be told before it happens. At Trailhead and Pony Express, the engineer explained why a line item budgeted at $500,000 — the bare cost of a traffic signal — was wrong: geometry problems, islands, extensive flatwork, repaving of some of the city's oldest asphalt, bulb-outs, a truck apron, and the removal of two rectangular rapid-flashing beacons that, in his words, "no one pays attention to anyway." Councilmember Clark, who joked about "support groups for residents that are upset about the traffic circle being removed," also asked the substantive follow-up: how will people know not to cross where the flasher is being pulled? The answer was that the deviating trail, the medians, and the striping all disappear with it, leaving no crossing to find. The engineer's philosophy surfaced most clearly on Mount Airy: "stop signs are not a great way to manage speed, or speed humps. The best way to fix speed is geometry" — widening the appealing route so drivers stop cutting through a neighborhood.

Several exchanges pointed toward policy rather than projects. Councilmember Wood, describing morning queues that back up past Ridley's and cascade from Pony Express onto Ranches Parkway all the way to Lone Tree, asked whether the city should adopt a broader policy restricting left-turn access from intersections and driveways, extending beyond Pony Express to Eagle Mountain Boulevard as it commercializes. The engineer agreed and said the development review team is already having those conversations — citing the Eagle Crest project, where staff objected to left egress. He also declined to add "do not block" striping to Pony Express, on the grounds that the underlying problem there is capacity, not driver behavior, "and if we do it there, we're going to want to do it everywhere." Wood separately invited staff to flag any project where a modest expenditure of one-time or utility funds would produce an immediate, widespread traffic benefit. Councilmember Wright offered unprompted praise: "It's super refreshing to me to see us proactively planning to fix some of the mistakes we've made in the past."

### 2.A — Airport Road right-of-way and pedestrian connectivity

After the Mayor excused the finance director and offered to adjourn, Councilmember Wood asked to continue, and the remaining time went to a question Councilmember Huish had raised privately with the Mayor. Airport Road is wide enough for two cars, but residents jog and walk along it, and Huish wanted to know whether the city owns enough land east of the road to build a roughly seven-foot pathway connecting the north and south halves of the city. The answer was no — the city holds the large parcel where Airport Road angles off Pony Express, but not the stretch going forward. Staff are currently preparing the legal descriptions to secure the additional right-of-way.

The engineer treated Airport Road as a lower priority on volume grounds — "it's a great secondary or third relief," valuable as redundancy when Eagle Mountain Boulevard is closed for paving, but not carrying the loads that Ranches Parkway and Pony Express do. Councilmember Wood accepted the assessment as honest while noting the discomfort of it: "it was a lot of money to build that road that nobody's using." Brad Hickman added the strategic caution that closed the meeting's substantive discussion — that dropping a trail in before the full road profile and adjacent development grades are known risks building it twice, "and those are some of the mistakes that we've made in the past too, where we've jumped the gun." A trail network is on the plans for the corridor; he would not commit to a timetable. The same widening question turned up at Pole Canyon Road, where Councilmember Wood asked whether the developer holding the west-side frontage — identified as Firefly — might build the roadway in exchange for something of value to them; staff reported hearing nothing from Firefly about developing that road anytime soon.
