## What was discussed

### 1.A — Concept Plan: The Bench
This was a work-session review of an early concept plan for "The Bench," a roughly 1,800-acre master-planned community proposed on land east of City Hall, toward Lake Mountain. Because city code caps any single master development plan at 200 acres, the applicant would need to file the project as at least nine separate master development plans reviewed together. Steven Lehmitz, the Planning Manager, told the commission staff is not comfortable with the overall unit count and placement, and wants the project to use the city's existing zoning tables and road standards rather than a custom set of standards built just for this site. Tom Romney, a planner and civil engineer with Focus Consulting representing the applicant, argued that a single large master plan lets the city negotiate amenities and infrastructure it couldn't get from a project built piecemeal over decades, pointing to Daybreak as an example of what unified large-scale planning can produce.

Romney's presentation centered on five "regional" amenities meant to serve more than just future residents of The Bench: a non-motorized trailhead, a large community park, an equestrian park with a riding arena and overnight horse boarding, a dog park, and a motorized-vehicle trailhead, all connected to Lake Mountain, which Romney said has roughly 3,000 feet of elevation gain, comparable to Park City Mountain Resort. The plan proposes roughly 9,000 housing units (a number commissioners briefly double-checked, since a slide appeared to show 91,000) at an average of five units per acre, mixing housing types throughout rather than clustering by lot size. Commissioner Allen said the plan "fits Eagle Mountain very, very well" and suggested relocating the equestrian trailhead closer to the project's eastern boundary so riders don't have to trailer through residential streets; Romney said natural drainage corridors running through the site already provide that kind of off-street route and offered to look at moving it.

Discussion also covered water and phasing. Romney said the landowner does not hold water rights for the full 1,800 acres and argued that requiring water to be secured or dedicated at the master-plan stage, rather than at platting, would make the project financially unworkable over a 30-to-40-year build-out. Amanda Mendenhall, representing the landowner, pressed the point further, noting some lots may be sold to builders who already hold their own water rights, and warned that an early water-dedication requirement would just push the city toward the kind of piecemeal development a master plan is meant to avoid. A commissioner (Speaker D) was sympathetic to phasing the project in roughly 200-acre pieces tied to platting, while staff (Marcus and Brandon) explained that the 200-acre cap exists mainly to size the city's review fee and workload, not to block large projects from being filed and heard as one cohesive application.

### 6 — Status report
Brandon, city staff, reported that the City Council had recently approved an artificial-turf code change and rezones for O'Callow and Parkway Crossing, and that the council and commission will see a revised residential water code back in early December after staff finalizes timing questions. ### 7.A — Pole Canyon Boulevard rezone to Commercial Storage / Business Park-Light Industrial
Planner Mickie Mills presented an applicant-initiated rezone and general plan amendment for about 10 acres on Pole Canyon Boulevard, split from a larger parcel in an earlier lot-split application. The land is currently zoned agriculture with a Neighborhood Residential future land use designation; the applicant wants Commercial Storage zoning and a Business Park/Light Industrial land use designation to build a self-storage facility. Mills noted the surrounding uses to the north, south, and west aren't technically "compatible" under the zone's reference table, but said this is a preference rather than a legal requirement since the decision is legislative. She added that a planned freeway realignment runs near the site and that staff expects minimal traffic impact, citing roughly 13 trips per day.

Chase Andreasi, representing applicants BAI and Candlelight Homes, said the facility would be built in two phases: self-storage units around the perimeter (doubling as a required fence, since the building wall itself meets the 6-foot fencing standard) and temporary RV storage in the middle until a second phase of self-storage is built out. He confirmed the project is Extra Space-branded and said the company runs extensive market studies before committing to a site. Commissioner Free, drawing on his work in real estate appraisal, said he supports the zoning change but cautioned that most new self-storage proposals he evaluates aren't actually financially feasible given existing supply, though he said this site "seems like you are" in a good position. No one spoke at the public hearing, and the commission forwarded a positive recommendation 5-0.

### 7.B — Development agreement with Ranch House Storage LLC
Marcus, city staff, walked through the development agreement tied to the 7.A rezone, covering the intended self-storage and outdoor vehicle storage uses, accessory office space, and the building-as-fence design along the property line. He confirmed the agreement sets a 19-foot front setback (a deviation from the standard 25 feet), which is allowed under the zone's code as long as the City Council finds the reduced setback won't harm adjacent properties' use or character. Commissioner DeCoursey caught a drafting error referencing the Salt Lake County Recorder's Office instead of Utah County, which staff agreed to fix before the item reaches the City Council. No one spoke at the public hearing, and the commission forwarded a positive recommendation 5-0 with that correction noted.

### 7.C — Alternative Animal Management Plan, Wilson Residence
Steven Lehmitz presented the city's first-ever application under its alternative animal management plan standards, adopted late last year, for a half-acre property in the Westview Heights neighborhood where the applicants want to keep up to two horses. Because the lot is only half an acre, horses aren't allowed unless the property gets an equine overlay zone (which Lehmitz said isn't ideal to apply to a single lot surrounded by non-equestrian zoning) or this alternative permit. Staff recommended a 5-foot buffer between the horse corral and the shared fence line and, in the packet, a minimum corral size of 2,500 square feet, based on the city's general livestock standard of 2,500 square feet per horse. The applicant, however, pointed out that the city's equine overlay zone sets a much lower standard of 600 square feet per horse, and Lehmitz acknowledged he intended to revise his recommendation downward but hadn't updated the materials in front of the commission. Marcus, city staff, said the two code sections are "clearly in conflict" and will need to be reconciled in a future ordinance amendment.

Applicant Melanie Wilson, appearing with her husband by phone from Hawaii, said the property sits across the street from an existing horse property and near a cluster of properties already keeping bison, goats, llamas, cows, and chickens, and that neighbors notified of the request (roughly 600 households, per staff) responded favorably. She said the family's goal is to keep two horses, proposing a 1,250-square-foot corral, above the overlay zone's 600-square-foot minimum but below the general code's 2,500-square-foot figure, plus a pole barn (already under building-permit review) for hay and tack storage. The chair pressed Wilson on her reference to a nearby "equestrian trailhead," clarifying for the record that the trail is private land owned by a neighboring HOA, not city property, and reminded her the approval would run with the current owners only, not the land itself. Commissioner Allen said the proposed 1,250 square feet comfortably clears the equine overlay zone's 600-square-foot standard, and the commission approved the plan 5-0 with staff's fencing buffer condition and the applicant's 1,250-square-foot corral.

## Notable moments
- **A 1,800-acre pitch, a 200-acre rule.** Tom Romney argued forcefully that breaking up The Bench to fit the city's 200-acre master-plan cap would sacrifice the regional parks, trailheads, and coordinated infrastructure that only come from planning the whole property at once, comparing the opportunity to Daybreak.
- **A landowner representative pushed back on water timing.** Amanda Mendenhall told the commission that requiring water rights to be dedicated at the master-plan stage, rather than at platting, would "only encourage piecemeal development" and asked the city to reconsider the policy direction it's been discussing at the Council level.
- **A commissioner's market-reality check.** Commissioner Free, speaking from his appraisal background, told the Pole Canyon storage applicants that "95% or more" of self-storage proposals he reviews don't actually pencil out given existing supply, even while supporting the rezone.
- **A recommendation changed mid-meeting.** Steven Lehmitz admitted he'd meant to lower his recommended minimum corral size for the Wilson horses from 2,500 to somewhere near 1,200 square feet after learning of the equine overlay zone's lower standard, but hadn't updated his slides, leaving the commission to sort out the right number live.
- **A light moment during roll call.** When calling the vote on the December meeting date, the chair called Commissioner Free's name twice, saying afterward, "Just seeing if you were paying attention."
