# Eagle Mountain City Council — member profiles

Private working notes, not site copy. Built from the 15 city council transcripts in
`data/transcripts/city-council/` (four from 2024, eleven from Feb–Aug 2026), their
summaries, and the 1,119-motion vote record in `docs/data.json`.

**How to read the confidence markers.** Six of the 2026 transcripts have a verified
speaker map (`*.speakers.json`), so quotes from 2026-04-14, 05-26, 06-02, 07-07, 07-21
and 08-04 are attributed on the record. The rest — the 2024 meetings, 04-28, 06-16 and
08-06 — have no reliable per-speaker split, so anything from them comes via the
meeting summary's named attributions, not raw diarization. Vote counts come from the
parsed minutes, which run back to 2019 and are the most reliable thing here.

## Zac Huish

**Seat.** Appointed 15 Jan 2026 to finish Jared Gray's unexpired council term, which
runs through 2027. Newest person on the dais by four years, and says so: "prior to
January of this year, I was totally oblivious to all the things that were happening."

**Record.** 100 votes, 95 yes, 3 no, 2 abstain (96.9% yes). Votes with Whiting 96% of
the time, Clark 95%, Wright 92%, Wood 78% — he is a middle-of-the-council vote who
occasionally breaks left of the majority on money.

**The one arc worth knowing.** He voted *no* on both the third FY25-26 budget amendment
and the FY26-27 interim budget and certified tax rate on 16 June 2026 — the only member
to oppose the interim rate. Then on 6 Aug he voted *yes* on the final property tax
increase, after opening with an apology ("we failed you" on communication), disclosing
he'd produced an explainer podcast at his own expense, and arguing the *advertised*
number was too high. His objection was to the size and the sales job, not the need. He
described the final rate as about 70 cents a day for an average household.

**Recusals — he is the most frequently conflicted member.** Recused from the $230M water
and sewer bond (Feb, work reasons), from the Sage Valley entrance sign ("this is my
neighborhood"), and abstained on the RDA sales tax pledge in June. He announces conflicts
before he asks questions rather than after: "I won't be voting on this issue, so I'm just
asking." Worth watching, but so far the disclosure has been early and unprompted.

**Signature issues.**
- *Technology in city operations.* His most distinctive push. Wants AI licenses for the
  city attorney and council in a closed system — he named Anthropic's Claude specifically
  — to review NDAs, contracts, and plans. "We use it in my daytime job... it saves us a
  ton of time." Also wants a better remote-meeting product than Lifesize and a Verizon
  tower near the city building.
- *Bitcoin for city application fees* (not utilities) — still on the legislative
  priorities list; would make Eagle Mountain the first Utah city to do it.
- *A split commercial/residential property tax.* Utah law forbids it; he has asked John
  Magnus to push the legislature. His framing: "my grandma living down the road in
  retirement, her property is not producing any kind of income."
- *Parks staffing and condition.* Pushed lower-cost crew positions ahead of directors,
  and wanted a wildlife biologist to replace Todd Black funded even though staff had
  ranked it below P2.
- *ADA access to park facilities* — recurring; the Sage Valley pavilion with no paved
  path from the sidewalk is his standing example (a neighbor's wheelchair had to be
  carried up).
- *Lone Tree egress to Airport Road*, and a ~7-foot pathway along Airport Road connecting
  the north and south halves of the city.
- *Medians and city character* — fought a rearguard action to keep some median on
  Ranches Parkway. "It's the entrance... I think medians make it interesting."
- *RV/trailer stacking* on half-acre-to-acre lots as an unpermitted business; wants it
  back in a work session.

**Fiscal instincts.** Invokes the "if, come" principle — don't commit to spending until
the revenue is actually there. Drew a hard line against paying recurring salaries from
RAP-tax money ("I really don't support using the RAP tax to pay for salaries"). Rebuilt
the entire budget himself: "I spent most of my weekend going back through every budget
session... copied every dollar that was presented, gave it a name, put it in a spreadsheet."

**How he operates.** Asks cost first, then process, then "is there any catch, anything
I'm not missing?" Reasons from small concrete anecdotes — three tree stumps left in his
local park, kids complaining the soccer field was too hard to kneel on, goats brought in
to clear open space. Quantifies things into daily-cost terms. Self-deprecating, and
apologizes a lot when calling in remotely (his audio failed repeatedly on 26 May; staff
ended up putting a phone next to the speaker).

**Personal, from the record.** Roughly 20-year resident, lives in Sage Valley. Works in
business — his company reimburses $60/month for cell phones and uses contract-analysis
AI; he says he's neither an attorney nor from a real estate background. His homeowner's
insurance was canceled despite living in a subdivision, which is why he asks fire-rating
questions. Daughter served as Miss Eagle Mountain Teen in 2019 (two years, because of the
pandemic). Once lived beside the bike park and confessed to "having PTSD remembering an
experience I had going off one of those ramps."

## Craig Whiting

**Seat.** Councilmember since 1 Jan 2026, term through 2029. First term.

**Record.** 110 votes, 108 yes, 2 no (98.2%). Highest agreement with Clark of anyone on
the council — 99.1% — then Huish 96%, Wright 94%, Wood 77%, Gray 75%. He is the council's
most prolific mover of motions: he made the motion on the Aug 6 tax rate, the annexation
policy plan, the youth council appointments, most closed sessions, and the Best Friends
partnership.

**The thing that defines him: he reads the documents.** More than anyone else on the
dais. On the Triumph deed restriction he came in with a list: "2.05 uses the word
conjunctive, and I believe it was intended to be injunctive," two sections numbered
3.06, two cross-references pointing at Article 5 that should say Article 4, "foreclosure
is misspelled in the last sentence of 7.02." He caught Jessie Call's name misspelled in
three places in the July 7 minutes. He also spotted the substantive trap in the same
document — that the city's power to modify the deed restriction under Article 4 must not
be delegable under 6.02 — and the drafting gap where an heir who inherits a
price-capped home would escape the owner-occupancy requirement.

**Signature issues.**
- *Zoning as leverage, held firmly.* His sharpest moment on the record: developers
  drawing residential plans for land west of Lone Tree that is still zoned agricultural.
  "The future land use map is not the gospel. The zone is... in my book, they can farm
  the land." Not hostile — he stresses the council is "collaborative, not punitive" — but
  he wants developers in front of the council *before* their road alignments become facts.
- *Precedent.* His recurring test. Opposed a 40-foot height exception for a church
  recreation building not on its merits but because "how would we be able to say no" to
  the next applicant. On the Triumph housing approval he put on the record that it "is
  not intended to set some kind of a precedence."
- *Discretion over entitlement.* Preferred an overlay zone to a special use for
  alternative energy, precisely because "if they meet the criteria, we can't deny it."
- *Youth council.* He's the liaison, and it's clearly the part of the job he loves —
  membership has roughly tripled; he moves every youth appointment personally and turns
  up to get dunked in the dunk tank.
- *The library.* Went out of his way to ask why a library position had been filtered off
  the staffing list, saying the library is understaffed by eight or nine people.
- *Contract hygiene.* Wanted cell phone contracts competitively re-bid every time they
  expire, credit-card reward points accounted for, the consultant list written as
  "including but not limited to," lobbying contracts to specify two meetings *per year*,
  and data on how much the city spends on outside counsel before hiring an in-house
  assistant attorney (whose $147K proposed salary he thought too high).
- *Volunteerism as a funding substitute* — cites a West Jordan park built by residents
  with donated materials. Same instinct behind the "I Believe in You Run Cedar Valley"
  half marathon he's organizing for Oct 3 with Cedar Fort, with proceeds to volunteer
  fire and police services.

**Voice.** Openly religious on the dais — he read the "Believer City" proclamation, urged
residents to watch Speaker Mike Johnson's prayer and read a scripture President Trump had
shared, and closed the tax hearing with the defense of Little Round Top at Gettysburg:
"Sometimes leadership requires leading people to a place they do not want to go because
we see a vision that we know we must obtain." Also the council's most explicit defender
of disagreement: "if you stay tonight, you're going to see probably 3 or 4 disagreements
... and that's healthy."

**Ethics note.** Recused himself mid-item on the Brylee Farms townhome discussion after
disclosing a small consulting project for the applicant a year earlier and a prior real
estate transaction with him — after he had already asked several pointed questions off
the MLS listings. The disclosure was voluntary but came a beat late.

**Personal, from the record.** Works in Lehi (his employer's office has a private road
where they installed a $7,000 speed bump — his evidence for cheap traffic calming).
Background in real estate: sold homes in Eagle Mountain "back in the day," served on the
Planning Commission before the council, checks the MLS before a hearing. Previously lived
in West Jordan. Gwen Whiting, the Cedar Valley internship teacher who appears in the June
2 meeting, shares his surname — the record doesn't establish a relationship.

## Brett Wright

**Seat.** Councilmember since 2022, re-elected for a term through 2029. **Announced on
6 Aug 2026 that he is in his fifth year and will not run again.** That's the single most
consequential fact in this file — his seat is open.

**Record.** 380 votes, 371 yes, 7 no, 2 abstain (98.1%). Agreement: Clark 96%, Whiting
94%, Huish 92%, Wood 84%, Gray 85%. His dissents cluster on land and money — against
annexing land from Cedar Fort (Oct 2024), against rejecting the inland port project area
(Apr 2025), against both the $95,000/acre land sale *and* the $45,000 alternative (Sept
2025), against the Hidden Hollow amenity swap being put on hold (Jan 2026), against the
Spring Run Plaza development deal (May 2026), against moving the noise curfew from 9 a.m.
to 7 a.m. (Apr 2026).

**What he's actually about: the utility of a thing, not its label.** The clearest example
runs across two meetings. A developer wanted to trade 600-square-foot garages for
496-square-foot ones. Wright's objection wasn't the number: it was that a "2-car garage"
with a staircase eating 18 inches is a one-car garage, a condition he's seen in his own
daughter's townhome. "They come in, they expect a 2-car garage to facilitate their needs
of 2 cars, and regularly it doesn't." When the developer came back in August, Wright's
answer was a trade — he'd accept the smaller garages if the *other* units got better
garage design, so buyers "will have access to a useful garage." Straight downgrades he
rejects: "If all you're going to do is change a 600-square-foot garage for a
500-square-foot... I don't care. To me, that's not a huge upside."

**Signature issues.**
- *Water, and a lobbyist to chase it.* His top legislative priority — "water and reuse
  water... as long as we keep our foot on the gas pedal. If we take that off, we're going
  to find ourselves being ignored." Attended a water conference remotely in June rather
  than miss it.
- *Public safety funding honesty.* He is the one who says out loud where the money
  actually went: only ~$2.8M of the sheriff's contract has historically come from
  property tax, the rest from sales tax and fees — "what does that mean to you as
  residents? It means your parks haven't been taken care of."
- *Staffing: boots over brass.* Repeatedly pressed for field workers, people "with a
  shovel and a wheelbarrow" to fix soccer fields and potholes, over an assistant city
  attorney, a payroll administrator, and a ~$147K–$200K community services director.
- *RAP tax discipline* — with Huish, refuses to let it fund recurring salaries.
- *Pragmatism on trails.* Backed the trails plan but warned "we're not going to preserve
  all of these trails. I know the mountain bike community doesn't want to hear that."
  Cautioned staff negotiating with SITLA: "let's make sure we're not giving away the farm."
- *Growth data skepticism* — challenged whether the MAG/WFRC model captures Saratoga's
  ~18,000-unit entitlement. "MAG is always behind."
- *Property rights over view protection.* On the satellite company asking the city to
  restrict neighboring buildings: "We don't buy the view, we buy the ground that we build
  on" — then offered to trade a height exception so they could raise their own antennas.
- *Against keeping medians on Ranches Parkway*, against Huish and Wood — maintenance
  crews get struck, water soaks the roadbed, and the corridor carries too much traffic.
  "It's too little too late."

**How he operates.** He is the council's procedural fixer. He rewrites motions so the
change is named in the text rather than implied (Whiting withdrew a motion so Wright
could restate it correctly on the noise ordinance); he offered the substitute motion that
separated the general plan amendment from the rezoning map; he volunteered on the spot to
sign the reconsideration letter on the stalled land disposal — "somebody on the staff
write that and I'll sign it" — which produced the three required signatures in real time.
He also plays mediator: during the Wood/mayor blowup over the senior housing overlay he
said "I want to intervene between 2 of my friends," twice, before giving up and moving to
table.

**Voice.** The most emotionally open person on the dais, and knows it — "I can get a
little booby, so I'm sorry about that." Spent the 4th of July alone in the park watching
16,700 residents and came back with: "who are you, Brett, to sit in this seat and to make
decisions for the 80,000 people that are impacted." Describes the job as "a loneliness to
leadership." Self-aware about his own weak spot: "I'm probably the worst communicator of
all, unless you sit down with me across the table, and then I'll communicate you to death."

**Personal, from the record.** In Eagle Mountain 22 years. Owns his home plus a rental in
the city. Four children, nine grandchildren; his 80-year-old mother, a 35-year-old
daughter and a 38-year-old son all live in Eagle Mountain — which is why he frames the
tax increase as landing on his own family. Liaison to the seniors group (organizing a
monthly food-pantry drive) and to the DRC, which he attends religiously. Rides an
electric bike; not a mountain biker. Wears a wig to the youth council dunk tank.

## Melissa Clark

**Seat.** Councilmember 2018–2021, then again since 1 Jan 2024, term through 2027. **Mayor
pro tem** — she chaired both sessions on 21 July when Gray was out, and ran them briskly
and by the book.

**Record.** 383 votes, 373 yes, 6 no, 4 excused (98.4%). The council's consensus center:
99.1% agreement with Whiting, 96% with Wright, 95% with Huish, 82% with Wood, 84% with
Gray. Her handful of no votes are telling — against the 2024 mayor and council pay raise,
against the $95,000/acre land sale and against the lower $45,000 price, against pausing
that sale, against rejecting a land disposal in April 2026.

**Her actual role: she is the council's institutional memory and its communications
conscience.** More than anyone else she tracks what the city promised, when, and whether
it happened. She remembers Sweetwater Park was "a pit" before 2017-18, that ten pavilions
got funded the year a public works bay expansion didn't ("we can do better"), that Parkway
Fields came back "eight times of not hearing 'no,'" that the Nolan Park splash pad has
never been made recirculating. Her AI proposal is exactly this instinct: throw all the
minutes and agendas into a model so the council can ask for a timeline of park
improvements by service area, "because we could say we did a big thing in Smith Ranch."

**On communication.** Her recurring diagnosis is that the city's problem is explanation,
not substance. After a candidate debate where candidates boasted about never raising
taxes, she "spent the drive home ranting to my husband" — and her takeaway was that even
he, who hears about it constantly, didn't understand the 55/45 residential reduction.
"So we probably need to do a better job of helping just everyone understand that." She
asked staff to walk residents through the budget process *on camera* mid-meeting, "so that
if residents go back later and watch it, they're like, oh, okay, that is something that is
a part of their process."

**Signature issues.**
- *Lone Tree egress, as both a problem and a warning.* Her sharpest sustained argument.
  Five summers of "we're working on it" with no dates: "if they could see that in 18
  months, this is what's going to happen, in 3 years, this is what's going to happen...
  the people of Lone Tree would be way more secure." And the general lesson: "Lone Tree is
  a cautionary tale for us... those mistakes are $5 million inadequate road mistakes, and
  I don't want us to keep doing that."
- *Boots on the ground before management.* Voted to defer the community services director
  a year: "what I care about is what the people see... take care of the people who are
  doing the job, and then we look at bringing in someone who coordinates the people doing
  the job. I'm not a no on this. I'm a wait." Floated outsourcing and a paralegal instead
  of a second full-time attorney.
- *The justice court and the master offense list.* Her standing conditions on public
  safety funding — the city can't enforce its own code without them, and the court has
  been discussed "for over 18 months and we're nowhere closer to it."
- *Social services.* Teen center at Rockwell High and its food pantry, utility assistance,
  a single place people can go for help, resume and interview workshops, the library
  pantry. Consistently the member who raises this.
- *Transit.* Chasing UTA, still sore about the bus route that fell through. Promoting the
  $2.50 on-demand service — "we want this to be wildly successful so I can say to UTA, I
  told you so, and then get us more routes."
- *Council embedded early, not consulted late.* Insisted two councilmembers join the
  transportation study from the start "rather than wait until the decisions are made and
  then say no."
- *Coverage over price* on the cell contract — she remembers the Verizon years, when staff
  in the building couldn't hold a call "if they weren't like standing on one leg with
  their hand in the air."

**How she operates.** Chairs cleanly and moves fast. Handles the process points others
miss — she announced from the chair which noticed items were *not* public hearings so
residents who'd got a notice wouldn't be caught out. Asks the question after the question
("where we're going to remove the flasher... what do we do to help people know that they
can't cross there anymore?"). Uses humour to defuse — "are we going to have support groups
for residents that are upset about the traffic circle being removed?" — and calls the
astronomy club "the nerds," as a term of endearment. When a lawyer hedges she pushes:
"Don't answer it like a lawyer. Answer it like somebody who's in a budget meeting."

**Worth flagging.** She has a role with the chamber of commerce and handles it openly. On
4 Aug, on the ladder-sign question: "This is me wearing a different hat... I will let you
guys communicate with the chamber staff instead of me about the signs, if that helps." She
then answered the code question in her council capacity. Handled well, but it's a
recurring dual role worth watching as sign and economic-development items come back.

**Personal, from the record.** Married 28 years as of late July 2026; she and her husband
saw the new Spider-Man for his birthday, which produced her framing of the tax vote:
"with great power comes great responsibility... one of the greatest powers that any human
could be given is to tax another human being." Spent a summer working alongside Public
Works and the city's purchasing agent on the wastewater treatment plant procurement, which
is why she defends that position. Has visited Cedar Valley High School for years and knows
the students. Liaison to the Community Services Board; also handles the American Legion
relationship. At the Aug 6 tax hearing a resident said she would spend her time getting
Clark and the mayor voted out.

## Rich Wood

**Seat.** Councilmember since 1 Jan 2024, term through 2027. Previously served on the
Planning Commission.

**Record — the outlier, and the most important number in this file.** 383 votes, 321 yes,
**28 no**, 1 abstain, 32 excused. 92% yes rate, against 98% for everyone else; he casts
roughly four times as many no votes as any colleague. His agreement scores are the lowest
across the board: 84% with Wright, 82% with Clark, 78% with Huish, 77% with Whiting, 74%
with Gray. He is also excused most often (32 times), so watch attendance separately from
dissent.

**What he dissents on.** Read the list and a pattern emerges — he votes no on things he
thinks the city is giving away or doing sloppily. Against the civic campus land purchase
(Feb 2025), against the downtown master plan (Mar 2025), against the Eagle Quest East
amendment (Mar 2025), against joining the Utah Valley HOME Consortium twice, against the
Harmony agreement amendment, against a historic zone, against fence and screening rules
three separate times, against the parks and trails master plan, against mid-year budget
amendments, against the citywide speed study, against adding animal-shelter resources to
the lobbying list. He voted no on the "newspaper" code update and once against a consent
agenda outright.

**His governing idea: entitlements are a trade, and the city keeps losing the trade.** He
says he was "pretty instrumental in writing the MDA code for that very reason, is to make
sure that we no longer became a city that was full of land brokers." Applied to Eagle
Quest East, whose developer wanted a third extension: "I would assume that if you're going
to come in and get an entitlement, that you have a plan to go that doesn't depend on
somebody else doing their stuff." When told denial would put the developer in breach
rather than let the agreement lapse, his answer was "Let them go into breach then." He
asked whether anyone would support the extension as written, got silence, and turned two
colleagues' willingness to table into a 5-0 denial. That is his method in one item.

**Signature issues.**
- *The 600-square-foot garage.* His fight, and he says so: "I was one of the people that
  fought for the 600 square feet." He commissioned the study of what vehicles Eagle
  Mountain residents actually drive — trucks and SUVs — and frames it as whether a family
  "can get out of the car, or does everybody in the car have to get out in the snow?" He
  is also fighting the same battle at the Legislature. He notes he is a real estate agent
  himself, so the argument isn't naïve about product.
- *Equal process for everyone.* Objected to discussing a church recreation building that
  was never noticed: "Every applicant should have the same route to be able to bring
  something before the council." And on the height exception: "Being a religious
  organization owning property shouldn't give you specific development rights" — then
  asked staff to measure school gyms and write a standard so the next applicant has a
  clear path. He is against exceptions and *for* rules, which is a different thing from
  being against the applicant.
- *Small modular reactors, in detail.* On the alternative energy overlay he wanted named
  technologies (he favors liquid metal fast and molten salt reactors, partly because
  byproducts could support a royalty agreement), geothermal stripped out until the council
  is briefed on injection and seismic risk, every administrative approval removed so
  elected officials keep the decision, the water-assessment waiver deleted, weed mitigation
  during grading, and decommissioning bonds escalated with inflation. This is the most
  technically prepared single intervention in the whole record.
- *Trail crossings and grade separation* — priced underpasses at $8–10M and asked whether
  to push the state to design them into the road instead.
- *Not over-regulating people's stuff.* Consistently the member who remembers cost falls on
  residents: on trailer parking, "we're adding additional cost to these people's monthly
  bills," describing $300/month storage on top of a mortgage. He killed the
  licensed-vs-unlicensed distinction as unenforceable and suggested the *garage-side*
  setback fix that resolved the corner-lot problem in one stroke. His framing to the code
  officer: "I want to make this easy for you, not more complicated."

**The July 7 blowup, worth knowing about.** He objected to being hurried on the energy
overlay, told the mayor a general-plan comparison was "kind of a low blow," and said
"we're not going to hurry through this because it's inconvenient for the staff... I'm
sorry, maybe that makes me an a-hole, but for me, I'm going to be an a-hole on this one."
Wright tried twice to mediate — "I want to intervene between 2 of my friends" — and
failed. His motion to table lost 2-3; the ordinance passed 4-1 over him; and then the
three colleagues who'd voted him down immediately asked staff to bring his remaining
amendments back. He lost the vote and won most of the substance.

**Also worth knowing: he defends the institution hard.** From the dais in May, on the
Silver Lake traffic petition — he lives in Silver Lake himself — he pushed back on
"people jump so quickly to conclusions of nefariousness," said nobody up there benefits
financially, that the Lake Mountain Corridor exists because of that council, that the
eight-to-ten-year timeline is a money problem and not a will problem, and invited critics
to call him rather than fight it out on Facebook. And on the tax, his is the sharpest
formulation anyone produced: **"we've been running cheaply, not conservatively."** He also
publicly apologized to a resident for the city's messaging: "you're not hearing what we're
saying. We're not saying it in a way that it's being understood... For that, I want to
apologize."

**Earlier, harder version of the same.** In April: "Other city councils did not have the
backbone to do it, and they're so proud of not raising taxes like that's some big beacon
to shine in the sky to say, I screwed all the future residents of this city, because
that's what's happened."

**How he operates.** Comes with a list and works it item by item. Demands citations —
when the city manager claimed liability exposure under the federal MUTCD standard for an
unstudied speed table, Wood said he'd called Horrocks Engineering and been told designing
one "takes like 15 minutes," and asked the city attorney to substantiate the claim:
"If we're going to make statements like that, it ought to be substantiated instead of
just throwing that out on the dais." Asks about succession and continuity ("gets hit by a
bus"). Prefers eating the elephant in pieces — proposed three separate meetings on trails,
parks and open space rather than one.

**Personal, from the record.** Lives in Silver Lake. Real estate agent. Former planning
commissioner — he sat on the body that first reviewed Eagle Quest East. Reads history for
context; took Whiting's suggestion and started watching documentaries on the founders.
Says the sewer and water rate increases "put a hurt on our family's finances" too.

## Jared Gray — Mayor

**Seat.** Councilmember 2020–2023 and 2024–2025, then **Mayor** from 1 Jan 2026 through
2029. Huish was appointed to finish the council term Gray vacated. He has been in the room
longer than anyone currently on the dais except Clark.

**Record.** His 277 recorded votes are from his council years — 236 yes, 10 no, 2 abstain,
29 excused (95.9%). As mayor he presides and does not routinely vote. His council-era
dissents were mostly about land: against the Berry property foothill rezone, against the
downtown master plan, against the inland port project area rejection, against two May 2025
rezones shifting land from business park to housing, against the $45,000/acre land price,
against holding over the mayor's-powers ordinance. He was, by his own account, the sole no
vote on an earlier rezone he still cites when developers invoke it. Lowest agreement with
Wood (74%), which tracks — the two of them clash most in the room as well.

**What he actually does in a meeting: he runs it.** His speech is overwhelmingly
procedural, and he is good at it. He restates other members' questions so applicants can
answer them ("Can I restate the question? I think what Councilmember Wright's asking is
..."), summarizes to test agreement ("I'm going to sum up what I've heard, and if I missed
something, let me know"), and converts loose discussion into direction to staff. He
protects hard stops, excuses staff who have commitments, and asks for a motion the moment
consensus is audible. If you want to know what the council decided without voting, read
his summaries.

**Signature issues.**
- *Traffic geometry, in unusual detail.* He argues with engineers about deceleration
  lanes and left-turn stacking depth using his own commute as evidence: "I actually use
  this left turn lane a ton. Whenever I'm going home, I use this a lot." He lost that
  particular argument, and accepted it.
- *Regional funding, which is his real leverage.* He sits on MAG and fights it openly over
  a reimbursement system that penalizes cities that prepay: "it's unfair to us who prepay
  to spend less." When Eagle Mountain roads scored below the funding threshold he said
  that "because of my just short of a tantrum" some may get moved up. His procedural
  instinct here is sharp — get projects into the impact fee facilities plan and the MAG
  list *before* building, because "once it's built, unless it's approved before it's
  built, MAG's off the table."
- *State preemption.* Asked members to oppose HB 184, which he said would treat
  subdivisions as approved if a city doesn't act in 30 days; declared the city "a big no"
  on Senator Sandall's bill redistributing data center tax increment. When Clark asked
  about a carve-out for high-growth cities, he said he'd rather kill the bill outright.
- *The freeway cliff.* His own added priority: SR-73 improvements end at Ranches Parkway
  in 2029, dropping traffic from ten lanes to two, and he wants frontage roads extended
  before that becomes a crisis.
- *Public safety phasing.* He produced the only concrete funding structure in the April
  budget session — four deputies as revenue arrives in September, four more mid-year in
  January, so the first budget carries half a year of salary while the ongoing cost is
  established.

**On the tax increase, which is his defining fight.** He heckled candidates at a public
debate who boasted of never raising property taxes — "every time I would holler out,
irresponsible. All you did was kick the can down the road for us to do it." His framing of
why costs rise is the clearest anyone gave: you are not paying more for *more* officers,
you are paying more for the same officer still covering your house, and the new residents
pay for the additional ones. He also told the council privately, per Whiting, that the
next four years would be the toughest because data center revenue has not matured, and
that he expects to be a one-term mayor even if he wants a second — because of the
decisions this term requires.

**Two things worth flagging.**
- He attended the 6 August truth-in-taxation hearing — the most consequential and hostile
  public meeting in this record — **remotely by video, citing a previous obligation**,
  while all five councilmembers sat in the chamber. The summary notes his absence was
  itself read as a comment. A resident said she'd spend her time getting him and Clark
  voted out.
- He is comfortable steering. He pulled the lobbyist's contract renewal forward off the
  consent agenda so it could be handled while the lobbyist was in the room; on the energy
  overlay he pushed to move past Wood's objections to reach other noticed hearings, which
  Wood took as a rebuke and said so. Both are defensible chairing decisions. Both also
  had the effect of shortening scrutiny.

**Voice.** Dry, fast, self-deprecating. Money instincts he describes as personal
conservatism — "a saver" who lives "on a ton less than what I generate" — but he argues
the opposite for public money, which should be put to timely use rather than sit and lose
value, citing utility funds now "worth half what it was." Distrusts assurances about land
protection because of a call he got from the state about petroglyphs being destroyed at a
nearby development. After a Shark Tank event at the junior high where a child asked for

not the person. Went on ABC News about the roads, was cut to a ten-second clip, and came
back irritated: "I didn't say that I was okay with 8 to 10 years... I'm not taking 8 years
for an answer."

## Reading the council as a body

**The blocs, from the agreement matrix.** There is a stable four — Clark, Whiting, Wright,
Huish — who agree with each other 92–99% of the time. Wood sits outside it at 74–84% with
everyone. Gray, in his council years, sat closer to Wood than to the four. Nothing here
looks like a faction fight; it looks like four consensus-builders and one member who makes
the council earn its votes.

**Almost everything passes 5-0.** Of 1,119 motions the overwhelming majority are
unanimous. The interesting thing is therefore *not* the vote — it's the work session,
where items get rewritten, tabled, or quietly killed before a motion exists. If you are
tracking influence, the summaries' "settled without a vote" sections matter more than the
decision tables.

**Where each of them adds value, in one line.**
- **Whiting** catches drafting errors and precedent traps before they reach the code.
- **Wood** refuses to let entitlements be renegotiated cheaply, and does the technical
  homework nobody else does.
- **Wright** fixes the motion, brokers the room, and asks whether the thing actually
  works for the resident who will use it.
- **Clark** remembers what the city already promised, and insists the explanation reach
  residents.
- **Huish** brings outside-sector practice (technology, finance discipline) and rebuilds
  the numbers himself.
- **Gray** converts discussion into direction, and works the regional and legislative
  layer the others can't reach.

**The through-line of 2026.** Every substantive fight this year traces back to one thing:
the city has been funding public safety out of sales tax and fees while property tax stood
still for two decades. Wood's formulation is the one to remember — **"we've been running
cheaply, not conservatively."** That deferral is why parks are understaffed, why Lone Tree
still has no second exit, why the water and sewer rates went up first, and why the tax
increase passed 5-0 on 6 August after a June vote where Huish broke ranks.

**Three things to watch.**
1. **Wright's seat is open.** He announced on 6 Aug that he will not run again. He is the
   council's procedural fixer and its most experienced member on development agreements;
   losing that is a real change.
2. **Huish's recusal rate.** Three conflicts in seven months, all disclosed early and
   unprompted — good practice, but a member who can't vote on water bonds, RDA pledges, or
   items in his own neighborhood is a narrower vote than the seat implies.
3. **Clark's chamber of commerce role.** Openly handled, but it recurs on sign and
   economic development items and is worth tracking as those come back.

**What the record can't tell you.** Occupations, party affiliation, campaign finance, and
outside business interests are largely absent — what's above is inferred from what people
said about themselves on a live microphone. The 2024 meetings and three 2026 meetings have
no reliable speaker separation, so the 2024 picture of Wright, Clark, Wood and Gray is
thin, and Whiting and Huish barely appear before 2026 because neither was seated yet.
